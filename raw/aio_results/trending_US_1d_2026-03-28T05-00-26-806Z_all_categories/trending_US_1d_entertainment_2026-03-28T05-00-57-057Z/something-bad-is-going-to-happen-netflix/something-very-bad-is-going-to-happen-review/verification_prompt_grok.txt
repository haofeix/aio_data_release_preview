As frustrating as it is that Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen opens with Rachel and Nicky’s wedding, then flashes back to “five days until ‘I do’” instead of being confident enough to tell a linear story, its cold open is the high point. The series is most immersive when it leans into the tension between society telling Rachel she has made the right choice and her body screaming at her that something is wrong; this claustrophobic, paranoid jolt emphasizes how much walking down the aisle can feel like an invitation to be judged, assessed, and sized up as prey or product. When the camera captures Rachel’s perspective looking down the aisle toward Nicky waiting for her at the altar, the guests’ chatter muffles and her breathing grows louder. The lighting is desaturated, the room shadowy, the attendees’ expressions inscrutable, even antagonistic. An unearthly growl rises in the sound mix and then the series cuts from a shot of Nicky readying himself to slide a gold band onto Rachel’s finger to hallways elsewhere in the house coated in blood. In that mode of atmospheric grimness and allusive dread, the series is agonizingly, horrendously tense, so much so that I couldn’t finish the premiere at night and had to watch the show exclusively during the day. Rachel and Nicky’s nuptials spark the narrative, but the series is most interested in the concept of a soul mate and how to stretch that idea to its literal extreme, and it slyly maneuvers its characters to explore tension within seemingly perfect relationships. If their bond isn’t sincere, selfless, and motivated by each others’ happiness over their own, then something very bad is going to … well, you get the idea. Boston knows the genre classics and layers them so liberally in the season’s first half that there’s no chance to catch your breath: Unseen visitors rattle doorknobs, footsteps skitter down hallways, reflections in windows and mirrors don’t sync with reality, and splatters of bright-red blood contrast with the series’ otherwise aquarium-green palette. Like The Night House, The Invisible Man, or Rebecca, Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen has a strong sense of how to unite the classic idea of a haunted house with a more modern version of a gaslighting man. But eventually it stops being suggestive and starts getting explicit, and by hand-holding its audience, the series falters. We live in a time of worrisome media illiteracy, and the show’s decision to dive so fully into “What actually happened was …” pedantry both pads its runtime and deflates its spookiness. This is not a performance problem — Morrone is skilled at undercutting her cool-girl casualness with anxiety and avoidance, and Wilbusch develops Nicky’s older brother, Jules, from a bullying brat into an obviously wounded failson. (Also deserving of praise: whoever in the makeup department chose Morrone’s lipstick shades. They’re beautifully garish.) This is a pacing and writing problem, and the series’ last three episodes especially struggle to maintain momentum. They’re the season’s goriest installments, and while they feature an early contender for the year’s most arousing TV sex scene, they’re also, thematically, the series’ flattest. Thanks to an onslaught of overly clarifying dialogue, all the tension the series worked so hard to build dissipates, like a balloon slowly losing air until it slumps to the floor, unable to bear its own weight. That’s too bad because Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen begins as a riveting remix of female-focused horror’s greatest hits. Horror films have long mined women’s experiences to explore how societies view otherness and monstrosity — menstruation and puberty (Carrie, The Witch), sapphic relationships (Heavenly Creatures, The Neon Demon), and motherhood (The Babadook, Under the Shadow) — and Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen does something novel by adding marriage to that conversation. It’s so confident in its argument that sometimes the most foreboding thing about heteronormativity is how customs and rituals are enforced upon us that we may not fully understand, and if we did, we probably wouldn’t agree with them. There’s an antisocial wish fulfillment in watching Rachel kick against Nicky’s family’s “This is how we do things” properness and a fascinating, underdeveloped suggestion that maybe Rachel and another relative of Nicky’s are the pair actually fated to be together. Condense the series’ depiction of love and marriage as a roller-coaster ride of eyes-squeezed-shut faith and stomach-clenching doubt into a two-hour movie, and Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen would be cooking all the way through. As it is, it’s a nervy beginning, a badass final sequence, and many minutes of domestic blisslessness in between. An all-timer of a movie lurks inside Something Very Bad Is Going to Happen, and the spookiest thing is that it exists in this bloated form. That was very fun and cool, and probably a bit of couples therapy for them being in the writers room. I’m the least cynical, but it’s very interesting to hear everyone’s perspective on marriage. The show touches on this, but a lot of it does come from your parents and the kind of marriage you were raised in, which I find very interesting. Thematically, that also ties into the idea of wedding tradition. So much of it has to do with the people who came before you. How did the Duffers get involved in this series, the first series they are executive producing not in the Stranger Things universe? So I had written the pilot script, and I pitched it to several producers. I was really excited to pitch it to them. I was glad they were interested enough to even hear me talk about it. They loved it and they came on board. They were incredibly supportive of me stepping into the showrunner role; it was their first time producing something they didn’t create. Their goal was: “We see you as creator and we want to uplift you.” They gave me a lot of great advice and maybe the best thing was that I have to stand behind every creative decision I make — don’t let someone else decide where the story’s going, you really need to follow a north star and stick to it, and that’s how you’ll stay true to the story. They are known for their love of horror and their film references, and there are so many in Something Very Bad. What were some of your inspirations, and did anything inspire from them? Carrie is a huge one. Rosemary’s Baby. I love the way that both of those movies take a major life event and put this genre bend to it, and really externalize the internal feelings you go through with these major life events. But I was also inspired by character dramas, like Who’s Afraid of Virginia Woolf? and The Celebration and Birth, and looking at interesting family dynamics and interpersonal relationships. Because, ultimately, the characters in the show are grounded and the emotion is grounded, and that was really important to me, especially if you’re going to watch a whole series. It’s not just about the horror. You need to be able to connect with these characters. That was something I talked about with the Duffers that I think they really excel at in Stranger Things — balancing the emotional story and whatever the horror is that these people are confronting. There is a lot of absurd comedy, but you said you wanted the tone to be one of underlying dread. In the beginning episodes, there is this unnerving feeling of being watched and not quite knowing what’s going on or what to believe. What was the biggest challenge with finding the show’s tone? I love that unsettling kind of disquieting tension and dread. A lot of it came together in the edit, figuring out when to release the tension and when to play it up. The most challenging thing is that the show feels so different in the first two episodes than it does in the rest, once it settles into itself. There was a lot of discussion about, “Are we going to scare people away?” The pilot has a lot of jump scares. The second episode is really tense, and then episode three is much more of a family drama. I think there’s enough character that you’re sucked in and there’s enough humor that you want to keep watching, even if you’re not a horror fan. But it’s definitely a bold choice to play with genres so much — episode five is this fun party episode and seven is a oner.
