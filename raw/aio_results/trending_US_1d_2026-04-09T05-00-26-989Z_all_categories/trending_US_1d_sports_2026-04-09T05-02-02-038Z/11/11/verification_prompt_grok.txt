Top Questions How did the concept of zero develop in India? How did zero spread from India to the rest of the world? What role does zero play in science and technology? zero, number denoting the absence of quantity. Represented by the symbol “0,” it plays a foundational role in arithmetic, algebra, computing, and scientific measurement. It lies at the center of the number line, separating positive numbers from negative numbers, and it operates as a placeholder in positional number systems. Though now ubiquitous, the concept of zero as both a symbol and idea is a relatively late development in human history. Although placeholder symbols for absence were used in earlier systems, the modern zero—as a numeral with its own value and arithmetic rules—originated in ancient India before spreading to the Islamic world and Europe. Historical development Early placeholders What Is a Placeholder?A placeholder is a symbol used in a positional number system to show that a digit is missing from a specific place. For example, in the number 102, the 0 acts as a placeholder to indicate that there are no tens in that position, while preserving the positions of the hundreds and ones units. The earliest known use of a placeholder in a positional number system appeared in Mesopotamia, where the Sumerians, about 5,000 years ago, used a slanted double wedge in cuneiform (system of writing in the ancient Middle East) to indicate the absence of a digit. A tablet from Kish (in present-day Iraq) from about 700 bce provides evidence of the use of three hooks to indicate missing digits within a number. Between the 6th and 3rd centuries bce, the Babylonians employed symbols—typically one or two wedges—to mark empty positions between nonzero digits in their number system. In ancient Greek mathematics the concept of nothingness was acknowledged but not formalized as a number. Greek philosopher Aristotle rejected the notion of a void and argued that division by zero lacked meaning, reinforcing the absence of zero in the Greek numerical system, which relied on the Greek alphabet and was limited to positive integers. The ancient Romans also developed a nonpositional numeral system, using letters such as I, V, and X for recording values rather than calculation. Arithmetic was typically performed on a counting board or an abacus, and even though Latin had terms such as nulla to denote “none,” there was no symbol for zero in Roman mathematics. The Maya civilization in Central America independently developed a place-value number system that included a symbol for zero, likely by the 4th or 5th century ce. They used a shell-shaped sign to show zero in calendar inscriptions and records. This symbol helped mark when a number position had no value, similar to how zero is used in modern numerals. In Mayan calendars days were counted from 0 to 19 rather than 1 to 20. Evidence of this system comes from buildings and carvings, as many written records were destroyed in the 16th century. The Indian invention The concept of zero developed in India over several centuries. The earliest known example appears in the Bakhshali manuscript, a birch-bark text discovered in present-day Pakistan. Radiocarbon dating by the University of Oxford places parts of the manuscript as early as the 3rd or 4th century. In this text a dot was used as a placeholder to indicate the absence of a digit in a specific place value—the earliest recorded symbol for zero. This symbol later evolved into the open circle used in modern numerals. By the 5th century mathematician Aryabhata described a positional system in his treatise Aryabhatiya. Although he did not employ a written symbol for zero, his system required a placeholder to differentiate numerical values based on position. In the 7th century astronomer Brahmagupta became the first known scholar to treat zero—referred to as shunya in Sanskrit—as a number in its own right. In his book Brahma-sphuta-siddhanta (628; “Correctly Established Doctrine of Brahma”), he defined rules for arithmetic involving zero, including operations with negative numbers. From India to the world Between the 8th and 9th centuries, Persian mathematician al-Khwarizmi studied Indian mathematical texts, including the works of Brahmagupta. In his treatise Algoritmi de numero Indorum (“Al-Khwarizmi Concerning the Hindu Art of Reckoning”), al-Khwarizmi described a positional system based on 10 symbols—1 to 9 and 0—and credited its origins to Indian scholars. His writings helped introduce the Hindu-Arabic numerals to the Islamic world. By the 9th century zero appeared in Arabic texts in a form derived from Indian notation, resembling the modern oval symbol. The system reached Europe by the early 13th century, after the Italian mathematician Fibonacci encountered it during his travels in North Africa. In 1202 he published Liber abaci (“Book of the Abacus”), a practical guide for merchants that introduced zero and positional notation to European readers. Despite its advantages, acceptance was gradual. Florence banned the use of Arabic numerals in 1299, for example, citing concerns about forgery.
