In recent years, the terms “unhoused,” “houseless,” “housing insecurity,” and “housing instability” have gained popularity in public conversations about homelessness. While most public discussions on homelessness are centered on single adults, many children, youth, and families also experience homelessness, and these terms are increasingly used to refer to them. We continue to receive questions from a wide range of people, from members of the media to coalition partners, about these terms and why SchoolHouse Connection primarily uses the phrase “experiencing homelessness.” In responding, our intent is not to tell people what to say or how to talk about homelessness. Language evolves, terms fall in and out of popularity, and people adopt the words they hear around them. We write here to explain the choices we have made, because language shapes perception, and perception informs action. The words we use influence how people understand an issue and what, if anything, they do about it. At SchoolHouse Connection, we have made an intentional choice to primarily use the phrase “experiencing homelessness,” rather than “unhoused,” “houseless,” or “housing insecure,” when communicating with the general public and to policymakers about child and youth homelessness. Here’s why. “I was only 9 years old when our house got foreclosed. I thought it was just going to be another couple of months and then we would have a house of our own. But that didn’t happen. Ever since then, I’ve kind of been house-hopping, couch-hopping and living in motels, doing what I need to do to live. I’m never houseless, but I’ve always been homeless.” – Paige, 18 “What’s Homelessness Really Like,” New York Times Our goal is to help the public better understand how homelessness is experienced by children, youth, and families; the protections and services provided by law; and the need for stronger policies and more support. Federal early care and education laws correctly recognize the reality of child, youth, and family homelessness, and therefore use a definition of homelessness that specifically includes children and youth who share the housing of others due to loss of housing, economic hardship, or a similar reason. It also includes those in motels due to lack of adequate alternative accommodations. This “hidden homelessness” is very common among children, youth, and families, due to lack of shelter space, as well as fear of child welfare and other authorities. In fact, more than 80% of children and youth identified as homeless by public schools are staying with others temporarily, or in motels, when they are first identified as experiencing homelessness. These temporary situations are very unstable, often unsafe, and put children, youth, and families at risk of harm. A number of studies find that children and youth who stay with other people temporarily or in motels have educational risks and outcomes that are comparable to or worse than the risks and outcomes for those who stay in shelters or are unsheltered. Early care and education agencies have specific requirements to identify and provide services to children, youth, and families who meet this definition of homelessness, but they can only do so once they have properly conducted outreach and training – and that means understanding the legal early care and education definition of homelessness. So we also stick with the phrase “homelessness” in our communications to the public because any other way of referring to the experience of homelessness would not track the terminology of the laws that provide critical protections and services for children, youth, and families experiencing homelessness. “Staying with others temporarily, nothing ever felt right. I always felt out of place or feared what would happen if I did something wrong or something happened that was out of my control. Phrases like “houseless” and ”‘unhoused may be intended as more respectful alternatives to “homeless,” but they are not inherently less pejorative. All three terms define people by a perceived lack. Moreover, in practice “unhoused”’ and “houseless” can contribute to the misconception that if someone has a roof overhead, they are not experiencing homelessness. That misconception makes our work explaining the reality of child and youth homelessness to policymakers and the public, including early care and education professionals, more difficult. These phrases reinforce narrow stereotypes about what homelessness looks like and who experiences it, pushing children, youth, and families even further out of national conversations about homelessness. “While yes, I had a house to sleep in, I had to walk on egg shells to keep it. At any point they could ask me to leave. I lived in constant anxiety.” – Anonymous “Housing insecurity,” too, is sometimes used to refer to “homelessness,” even when situations that are being described very clearly meet the early care and education legal definition of homelessness. The term “housing insecurity” can minimize – and often seems to be intended to minimize – the harsh, traumatic realities of families and youth who are staying with others temporarily or in motels. The term may be used by proponents of the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development’s narrow definition of homelessness as a form of triage to relegate children, youth, and families to a second, lower tier for responses to homelessness. This term also can be confusing and misleading for early care and education professionals, who have specific responsibilities for identifying and serving children and youth who meet the legal definition of homelessness (not “housing insecurity”) for early care and education programs. There are instances where “housing insecurity” is defined in a way that is meaningfully different from “homelessness,” and indicates a level of hardship that falls short of homelessness. For example, organizations such as the Hope Center and Trellis Strategies use “housing insecurity” alongside a separate, broad definition of “homelessness” in surveys of institutions of higher education. When referring to the findings from these surveys, we use the terms as defined. The terms “houseless,” “unhoused,” and “housing insecurity” put an emphasis on housing as the sole condition to be addressed. But homelessness is a complex phenomenon with many contributing factors and different kinds of instability. For children and youth, homelessness is the lack of a safe and stable place to live, grow, and thrive. Its causes are complex, and may involve factors that are unrelated to the lack of affordable housing – factors such as domestic violence, parental addiction or mental health challenges, and/or abuse and neglect. A national study found that youth and young adults at the greatest risk for experiencing homelessness were those who lacked a high school degree or GED; were single and pregnant or who have a child; and those with low-incomes (in that order). Implicitly reducing homelessness to a narrow housing problem inaccurately conveys the complexity of homelessness, and has led to misguided attempts at solutions that address only one symptom, rather than multiple root causes. The results of “housing only” policies may have the unintended effect of exacerbating homelessness by leading to evictions, trauma, and unaddressed root causes. Finally, we use the phrase “experiencing homelessness” because of the many children, youth, and parents who have told us that homelessness is something that happened to them – it does not define them. It is something they went through, it is not who they are. The phrase “experiencing homelessness” emphasizes this powerful insight from young people and parents. It conveys that children, youth, and families experiencing homelessness are first and foremost children, youth, and families. Homelessness is a situation that they are in – that they are experiencing – and while it creates tremendous barriers, it can be overcome, and collectively, we can pursue multi-faced policies to solve it.​​ Addressing Stigma and Shame in Communicating with Families and Youth We do recognize the stigma and shame that the word “homeless” brings, particularly for children, youth, and families. We know that the word itself creates barriers to identification and services. But “unhoused,” “houseless,” and “housing insecure” are equally stigmatizing, with their emphasis on deficits. In fact, all of these phrases have negative connotations because homelessness is a negative experience. When the purpose of communication is to help the public, policymakers, and early care and education professionals understand homelessness, euphemisms should not be used to obscure the harsh realities. To avoid the stigma of any of these phrases when speaking to children, youth, and families, and when conducting outreach, skilled early care and education professionals avoid the word “homeless” and other phrases altogether. These professionals use phrases that describe the actual living situation, rather than the person. In a school setting, they may use phrases like “Students in Transition” or “Students in Temporary Housing,” or give their programs names like “Project Connect” or “Project Access” or “Project HEART.” Some educators may refer to “McKinney-Vento students” as a way to call attention to the law without using the phrase “homeless.” Ultimately, what we do about homelessness matters more than the language we use to describe it. But language shapes our perceptions and understanding, and therefore our actions. So we choose our words carefully, considering who we are communicating with, and the purpose of our communication. The questionnaire was translated into French using the back-translation method for French-speaking participants (Citation61). Individual interviews were then conducted using the life history mapping method, a biographical approach designed to visually chart participants’ life trajectories and capture the complexity of their life courses (Citation62,Citation63). Emphasis was placed on ABI related needs that influenced residential pathways and service use. Probing questions were used to explore participant’s service use throughout their life trajectories. This may have included questions such as, ‘Can you tell me about the people who had a positive or negative influence during that period?,’ ‘Can you tell me about the help or services you received after this incident?,’ and ‘Can you tell me if there were other supports you would have liked during that period?’ The full interview guide is provided in Appendix A. If participants did not spontaneously mention the ABI or services received when discussing their life trajectories, the interviewer asked service-related questions based on the results of the OSU-TBI-ID questionnaire. Overall, the data collection process averaged 75 minutes per participant.Data analysisAll interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim. The transcripts were then subjected to thematic analysis using Paillé and Mucchielli’s framework (Citation64). All stages of thematic analysis were conducted by the lead author [WJ], a doctoral student in rehabilitation sciences trained and experienced in qualitative analysis, and the study’s second author [LR], a researcher with extensive experience in qualitative methods. Although the lead author [WJ] was involved in conducting the interviews, the second author [LR] did not participate in data collection. This reduced the risk of analytical bias and ensured that the preliminary analyses did not influence the interview process. After thoroughly and repeatedly reading the transcripts, units of meaning were identified and thematized through line-by-line analysis. While the companion article focused on identifying turning points in the participants’ life trajectories, this analysis concentrated on their met and unmet needs in their daily lives and across the different services involved in their care. The themes were grouped into broader categories by identifying similarities, complementarities and differences among the data. An inductive thematic tree was constructed to structure the relationships between the categories and highlight the major themes and sub-themes. These thematic groupings were then presented to the research team for discussion and refinement, which allowed the three main themes presented in this paper to emerge.Member checking interviewsTo enhance the credibility of our findings, we sought to reestablish contact with all participants who completed the initial interviews to conduct member checking interviews (Citation65,Citation66). The recruitment process mirrored that of the initial interviews. All individuals who could be reached agreed to participate; however, several could not be contacted again due to various reasons such as disengagement from partner organizations or incarceration during the interim period. All member checking interviews were conducted by the lead author of the study [WJ]. The main themes that emerged from the thematic analysis were presented to the participants for verification of their accuracy in reflecting the participants’ actual opinions and perspectives on the issue. Participants were also encouraged to report on anything relevant to the study’s objectives that had not been identified in the analysis. These validation interviews confirmed that the results were meaningful and accurate from the participants’ point of view. In a few cases, they also provided additional insights that deepened our understanding of the significance of these themes in the participants’ lives. Incorporating this feedback further reinforced the credibility and trustworthiness of the analysis.Ethical considerationsThe research project was approved by the Research ethics committee on addiction, social inequalities and public health (project number 2024–2031). To ensure anonymity, participants were asked to choose a pseudonym that would be used to identify them throughout the research process. The names presented in this article are the pseudonyms chosen by the participants. For more information and precisions concerning ethical considerations, please refer to the companion article (Citation1).FindingsTwenty-six participants were interviewed, 16 males and 10 females. Participants ranged in age from 21 to 68 years and reported an average of 4.7 ABIs. All participants (100%) sustained a first ABI before the onset of homelessness. Using the ETHOS classification (Citation58), participants’ housing trajectories reflected a broad spectrum of homelessness and housing exclusion experiences, including living rough (category 1), staying in emergency accommodation (category 2), residing in temporary accommodation for the homeless (category 3), staying in women’s shelters (category 4), receiving long-term support due to homelessness (category 7), and living in insecure accommodation (category 8). Sociodemographic characteristics and general overview of sustained ABI.Three main themes emerged from the thematic analysis: 1) Intersecting health and social challenges and needs related to ABI and comorbid conditions influence residential pathways, recovery, and service use; 2) Fragmented and often inadequate service responses to complex and intersecting needs and challenges and 3) Non-use of health care services.Theme 1. Intersecting health and social challenges and needs related to ABI and comorbid conditions influence residential pathways, recovery, and service useParticipants reported various challenges present in their daily lives as a consequence of their brain injury, as well as of other mental and physical health conditions. These experiences, while not always explicitly expressed as needs, revealed significant areas where support was required. These combined challenges created needs for health, housing, and accommodation services.ABI-related needs and challenges that influence recovery and residential pathwaysSeveral participants reported the functional impacts they experienced in daily life due to the cognitive, motor, and sensory consequences of their ABI. While participants often described these impacts as symptoms or difficulties, they implicitly pointed to unmet needs for adapted support and rehabilitation. Memory loss, difficulties with sustained attention, organization, and problem solving, sensory hypersensitivity, chronic pain, fatigue, and motor and language difficulties were the consequences that emerged most strongly from participants’ interviews. Tintin, a man in his early sixties, was recovering from repeated strokes when he experienced homelessness for the first time. He reported how memory losses affected his ability to navigate shelter life:But there’s something that’s not working. I know there is. I know something isn’t working. Because sometimes I can even go up [to the locker space] four or five times when I’m going upstairs and say, what was I looking for? – Tintin.Moss, a trans woman in her early twenties, reported significant memory loss following multiple ABIs during her childhood and teenage years:I know I’ve been affected brain wise because I have no … I have no memory. I don’t even know what day it is unless someone tells me, even if I knew it earlier in the day. – Moss.Some participants also noted the affective and mental health consequences they attribute to ABI. This is the case for Kim, a woman in her mid-fifties who sustained multiple ABIs in recent years in the context of intimate partner violence:I feel like it affected me in that it made me more … depressed. Depressed, confused. Like I didn’t know what decision to make, and I was self-doubting about the decisions. And I think that came from getting hit in the head too much. – Kim.These various consequences of ABI create functional challenges in the daily lives of participants, particularly in the areas of social interactions, financial management and community navigation. Although these were described as challenges or difficulties, they reflect underlying needs for cognitive, emotional and social support. Functional limitations related to engagement in leisure and work-related activities were also noted. Social interactions were particularly challenging for participants, due to behavioral difficulties, including impulsivity, aggression, and irritability, compounded by cognitive difficulties and, for some, impaired communication skills. This was the case for Égide, a man in his early twenties, who saw radical changes in his behavior following multiple ABIs, affecting his capacity to interact with housemates:You know, before, I had trouble expressing myself. It was hell. So, you know, I was the complete opposite. I would never tell you I was angry, I would never say there was a problem. I avoided everything. And now, like, I find myself super easily in confrontation, being impulsive, and just saying whatever I’m thinking. – Égide.A similar pattern emerges for financial management, where the confluence of cognitive challenges and impulse regulation creates difficulty in planning and following a budget. Max, for example, acknowledged that his impulsivity has had a major impact on his financial management and, ultimately, his ability to maintain and obtain housing:Well, I had a problem with budgeting. I didn’t always pay my rent on time. [Interviewer: You were evicted from housing?] Yeah. [I had] trouble prioritizing. I really liked going out partying in bars and all that — I pretty much put that first, but really that should’ve come last. […] And then … a guy’s got a place for rent, and there are ten people who want it, and he sees that I’ve always had trouble paying my rent. I’m already starting with three strikes against me. – Max.Multiple and complex health and social needs and challengesThe majority of participants reported complex and multifaceted needs relating not only to their ABI, but also to other physical and mental health conditions. Many reported challenges associated with various mental health issues, including substance use disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder and mood disorders. Additionally, participants often experienced various physical health issues beyond their ABI, such as chronic pain, musculoskeletal conditions and diabetes.Several participants discussed significant needs specific to mental health issues. These needs persist despite residential stabilization, as Mr. Toujours, a man in his early fifties who recently obtained permanent housing with support explained:I knew it wouldn’t change anything [when I got permanent housing], you know. Well, in terms of my mental health, it wouldn’t change anything […] With my borderline personality disorder, I can feel sad and lonely more intensely than normal people […] I’d like to see the psychiatrist more often […] It always feels good to talk about personal things, about what’s going wrong and what’s going right. – Mr. Toujours.Max, a man in his late fifties who recently obtained a bipolar disorder diagnosis spoke about the need for close medical monitoring and management to help him function better on a daily basis:During my ‘highs’ [manic episodes], I could go four, five days without … like a guy on drugs, four, five days without sleeping […] You know, when I feel impulsive, it was during my ‘high’ moments […] If they had detected my bipolar disorder earlier, and given me medication, you know, I wouldn’t have had those moments of rage — I call it my rage — and it would have saved me a lot of problems. – Max.Many participants also discussed issues relating to emotional trauma and abuse, such as physical, psychological, or sexual assault, as well as adverse childhood experiences. These experiences, while described as psychological or behavioral challenges, also highlight the need for trauma-informed and sustained psychosocial support. Raphaël, who is in his mid-thirties, spoke about the frequent flashbacks he still experiences from childhood traumas, and the resulting behavioral consequences:It keeps coming back to me sometimes. I’m trying, but it keeps coming. Unexpectedly, it comes back to me—what happened in my past life. I almost went crazy this morning, you know? I get mad at my friends sometimes out of nowhere. – Raphaël.Several participants mentioned needs related to the use of psychoactive substances. For many, substance use is a significant challenge and is connected to many of the difficulties they have encountered. Kevin talked about his problematic substance use:I used [drugs] for a hell of a long time. Sometimes I wonder why I’m still alive today. I think back on it sometimes and say, ‘Christ! I should be dead with all the drugs I’ve taken in my life.’ It definitely doesn’t help my situation either. – Kevin.For Ali, a man in his early sixties who has struggled with substance use disorder for several years, the needs associated with his addiction pose a significant challenge to his daily life and quality of living. Although he is willing to do anything to stop, he requires assistance:I always go back to it [substance use]. I always go back to it. I always go back to it. And nothing … until I get to that point, nothing can bring joy to my life. Nothing. Nothing […] I’m weak, very weak, fragile. Everything that happens to me, I run to drugs […] I would ask the doctor to take my eyes, if that could make me quit drugs. This is how bad it is. I’m willing to go blind with no … in a wheelchair too, with no legs. Just make me quit. How? That’s the million-dollar question. – Ali.Characteristics of community and housing environments restrict participation and recoveryThe challenges and consequences associated with ABI are compounded by the characteristics of community environments, including shelters and community spaces. Such community environments are often characterized by loud noises, artificial lighting, strong smells, and proximity with other service users who may display challenging and unpredictable behaviors. Égide described how these factors contribute to his behavioral challenges:Because I have more of a concentration barrier, well, when I’m in really stimulating environments like the day center, where sometimes there’s two different kinds of music, people are talking, shouting, there’s a lot of smells, the light is bright, the more stimulating the environment, the more it interferes with my own thoughts. And then, because I’m having trouble thinking, I get kind of frustrated. – Égide.Other participants emphasized their inability to recover after being discharged from the hospital after an ABI because of the living conditions in shelters, and that they needed a calm living environment to aid their recovery. Their accounts suggest an implicit need for supported housing environments that accommodate cognitive recovery. This is the case for Catherine, a woman in her late sixties, who rapidly got discharged from the hospital following a brain surgery related to her ABI. She spoke about the difficulty of recovering in an emergency shelter:[…] They put you in a room where the girl doesn’t sleep all night, and then she sleeps during the day. And then I need to sleep, and then I have trouble sleeping. You know what I mean? […] It’s not suitable [recovery] with someone who doesn’t sleep at night, who keeps you from sleeping. – Catherine.Finally, several participants spoke about their prospects in terms of housing and accommodation, emphasizing the need for their housing to be adapted to their complex needs related to ABI and other traumatic life events. In some cases, participants expressed these as fears or safety concerns rather than direct service demands, but they nevertheless reveal a need for secure, adapted housing. For example, Minecraft99, a woman victim of intimate partner violence for several years, spoke about the need for high-security housing:I’m scared to death now. Sometimes I’m okay in a place like this [shelter]. Then, when I’m outside, I realize I’m smoking a cigarette, then there’s no one around, then, even though I’m a chronic smoker, I put out my cigarette, then I run inside. Then I ring the bell. Then sometimes I’m in the bathroom, then I’m scared. I’m scared now. I didn’t have that before. You know, when you say you’re at peace, you’re not afraid of anything. But now, I’m afraid. Even in women’s places. It’s crazy, right? – Minecraft99.What Moss really wants is a permanent home where she can isolate herself and rest and recover from her ABI:Just a roof over my head. Then I can stop talking to everybody, then I can go to bed, then I don’t go out for six months. Sleep. – Moss.Therefore, participants present complex social and health profiles, with multiple needs that require coordinated, cross-sectoral responses throughout the different services involved in their care trajectories.Theme 2. Fragmented and often inadequate service responses to complex and intersecting needs and challengesThe various services involved in caring for people experiencing homelessness with ABI, in both community and institutional settings, respond differently to the challenges and needs of the participants, whether relating to their ABI, mental health, housing situation, other medical and social conditions, or all of the above.The response of community and frontline services to complex health and ABI-related needs and difficultiesMost of the participants we met reported a positive relationship with at least one community worker or health care professional from specific teams in the health and social services network, including homelessness outreach services and frontline mental health and addiction services. Many participants described the community environment as welcoming, conducive to building trust, and able to meet a wide range of their needs. These positive connections have even enabled several participants to obtain permanent housing at some point in their lives.I have people here to help me. [They help me with] anything that has to do with family problems, whatever, if I need to take steps or whatever. Yeah, they do everything. […] It can be anything from applying for welfare to …, whatever you want. No real limits here. – Bob.Many participants said that, after several years, they had managed to build important connections with their support workers, despite a lack of trust in most people and considerable reluctance to connect with others because of the adversity they had experienced over their lifetime:Before, I didn’t talk to anyone. It was impossible for me to confide in anyone. You see, I have zero trust in the world. I have zero. I’ve had such a shitty time. So, I don’t trust anybody anymore. And then, shit, [my community workers] I’ve known them for a long time. I’ve known them for almost ten years. I’m [only now] starting to talk to them. – Kevin.However, while these community and frontline services recognize participants’ health-related needs, including the cognitive and behavioral consequences of ABI, they are neither designed nor sufficiently equipped to address these needs. This mismatch can result in key needs going unmet and lead to fragmented care, and even, exclusion from these services. Catherine, for example, talked about her perception of the lack of training for community workers:I understand that in places like this [homeless shelter], there aren’t specialized people who … well, not specialized, but who have training in care. And it’s not just brain injury. Whether it’s any kind of care, I find that the training is too technical and too regulated, which means that they have difficulty adapting to specific cases. – Catherine.Conversely, some participants have been excluded from community or frontline health services because of their challenging behaviors:After psychiatry, they put me in therapy. It was a six-month mandatory therapy, but I didn’t finish my six months. They thought I was too ‘speedy’ — not on the drug speed, but I was too hyper — so they transferred me here [to the hospital]. – Matthew.The response of specialized ABI services to social vulnerability and housing instabilitySeveral participants had access to specialized services relating to their ABI and reported being satisfied with the emergency services for ABIs, which they described as quick and efficient. For instance, many participants had access to brain scans and consultations with neurologists as part of emergency hospital consultations. Some patients with severe injuries also had access to rehabilitation services specific to their ABI. However, this initial positive experience contrasted with the lack of follow-up by the health network after this initial episode of care, despite the presence of significant persistent symptoms:[Interviewer: But you don’t think there’s any follow-up after that?] No, no, no, no, no. I was the one who called. I was the one who called. I said, ‘Hey, what are the results?’ ‘But you’re still under investigation.’ “Yes, but under investigation? I said, “What do you mean it’s not moving any further? I said, ‘If it was your son, would you move more?’ He said, ‘We’re not talking about my son, we’re talking about you.’ But I said, ‘Exactly. That’s the wrong answer. The day it’s your son, I’m sure things will move.’ – Tintin.Participants with severe injuries who were admitted to hospital-based or rehabilitation center-based ABI-specific services identified those services as poorly adapted to their social situation or mental health status. These situations resulted in early discharge and loss of follow-up from ABI-specific services, either through exclusion of services or when participants refused further involvement in care. This was the case for Buck, who returned to an emergency shelter after an early discharge from a rehabilitation center with no follow-up despite significant residual effects following a severe traumatic brain injury:I was in rehab [for my ABI] and I went outside to smoke a cigarette and it was windy. And I smoked my cigarette and all. And I don’t remember throwing it out. Anyway, to make a long story short, I went back into the place [the rehabilitation center], and I was starting a fire. The cigarette had been blown onto me, and … so I got kicked the hell out! – Buck.The (lack of) response of general health services, criminal justice services and correctional services to ABI-related needs and difficultiesMost participants reported that they did not have access to screening, assessment and referral services for their ABI in certain services, notably general health services, youth protection and criminal justice or correctional services. Some participants also spoke about the lack of training on ABI for workers in these settings and the failure to address their ABI-related needs. This is the case for The chosen one who deplored the lack of consideration for his ABI and the state of his mental health in the prison system:They should have sent me straight to [a mental health institution] to be treated by people who knew what they were doing. You can see in my file that I’m not a criminal. I’m not a drug dealer, a gangster or anything like that. Those filthy dogs in the prison system don’t understand a damn thing about rehabilitation. Rehabilitation means not turning people into criminals or killers. At [the mental health institution], they know how to treat mental health problems. They know how to talk to you and treat you. Even if they get angry with you, they’ll send you to your room, but they’ll come back and talk to you. You see, that’s what that place is for. The [prison] system? What even is that? – The chosen one.For many participants, the lack of recognition of ABI meant that they had very difficult experiences in services and received interventions that were not tailored to their needs. Some participants who had symptoms of impulsivity, hypersensitivity, and difficulty adjusting to the prison environment because of their unrecognized ABI experienced crises that were sanctioned by placements in segregation:There’s a problem in the prison system, there’s a big problem. How are they dealing with it? Look, I’ve never robbed a bank, so why are they treating me like this? A week and two days before I take a shower, sleeping on sheets that are full of shit? I was treated like … a dog is treated better than that. – The chosen one.When participants, like Égide, tried to access general health services to manage ABI-related symptoms, all their symptoms were attributed to substance use:Well, he [the family doctor] thought I was like a junkie and that I wanted drugs. Nonuse of health care servicesSeveral participants spoke about their tendency to avoid using health services, for either urgent or non-urgent health needs, but also after a blow to the head or in the presence of symptoms related to a non-traumatic ABI. Several reasons were given by participants to explain this avoidance of services, including previous negative, non-helpful or traumatic experiences in the healthcare network:Yes, I stayed at home [when I had symptoms of stroke]. But let me tell you what they do at the hospital. They run some tests and then leave you in a fishbowl. Yes, they saw me fairly quickly every time. But still, nothing happens afterwards, you know? – Tintin.There were bars outside [the psychiatric institute]. You’d go downstairs and see fencing. It was like a real prison – a maximum-security prison, I’m telling you. It was the worst time of my life. It’s no joke – you had to keep an eye on everyone, otherwise they’d hit you or beat you up. – Poupou.However, these negative and traumatic experiences are not always solely attributed to the care and services that the participants received themselves. Rather, they are intertwined with lifelong adversity, including memories of loved ones being seriously ill or dying, and of the difficult hospital experiences surrounding these events. This makes it challenging to disentangle the painful memories from the services themselves:I have a phobia of hospitals. I have to push myself to go. I hate hospitals. […] My youngest daughter was born prematurely. She was born at 24 weeks. […] We brought her home 26 months later. I would finish work and go to the hospital. I slept at the hospital, I ate at the hospital, I basically washed myself at the hospital. We were always, always at the hospital. […] I already had issues with hospitals. When I went to the hospital, I always wanted to go with someone. I didn’t want to go alone. But now, I don’t want to go at all anymore. I want nothing to do with hospitals anymore. – Kevin.Other participants, like Ali, talked about the belief that their health would not improve or, worse, would deteriorate if they sought health care services to explain this nonuse:I don’t like hospitals […] Sometimes you’re not sick, you go to the hospital, you get sicker. I have this mentality, which is wrong, but … – Ali.Several participants also talked about the difficulty of tolerating the hospital environment because of the long waiting times involved, nonsmoking and no-use of psychoactive substances rules, the lack of access to meaningful activities, and the overwhelming sensory environment, including loud noises and bright lights:I went to the hospital, but I didn’t sign in. Too many people. Too many people. You get there, it’s full […] And you’ll see, people wait for twenty hours […] So, I don’t feel like waiting. – Tintin.I don’t want to go to the hospital. You can’t smoke, you can’t go out, you can’t do anything. – Poupou.Finally, pride in being able to cope on one’s own and not asking others for help was often cited as a reason for not using health care services, by male participants only.Look, I’m someone who never asks anything from anyone. Never, never, never, never will you see me begging anyone for anything. Never. […] Me, having a doctor for a health issue … look, I’m totally fine. I don’t ask anything from anyone […] I don’t want [help with] anything, nothing at all! – Buck.Overall, the results highlight three interrelated themes. Firstly, participants described the cognitive, emotional and social consequences of ABI, as well as other health and social challenges that shaped their daily lives and interactions with services. Secondly, they reported fragmented and often inadequate responses from the various services involved in their care, reflecting gaps in coordination and expertise. Thirdly, several participants described a pattern of disengagement or avoidance of services, which was often rooted in previous negative or traumatic experiences.DiscussionThis study identified multiple interrelated challenges, as well as met and unmet health and social needs among people experiencing homelessness and ABI in their daily lives and across various services involved in their trajectories, delivered in both community and institutional settings. The findings also revealed a fragmented and often inadequate response from the various services involved in participants’ care trajectories, as well as the importance of addressing the functional challenges that contribute to the onset and persistence of homelessness in a transactional manner that considers individual and environmental factors. The findings demonstrate that the cognitive, emotional and behavioral consequences of ABI, such as memory loss, impulsivity, fatigue and emotional dysregulation, interact with other health and social vulnerabilities, including mental health issues, trauma and substance use. When these intersecting challenges coincide with unstable housing conditions and fragmented service systems, the result is repeated episodes of service exclusion, homelessness, and disengagement from care. However, participants also described how establishing trusting relationships with community workers and having access to stable, supportive housing could promote recovery, stability, and re-engagement with services. We acknowledge that some of the findings reflect challenges or difficulties rather than explicitly stated ‘needs.’ In line with prior research on unmet needs in vulnerable populations, we considered these challenges to be indicators of areas in which services could intervene, even if participants did not express them as demands (Citation35,Citation67). This approach enabled us to capture both expressed and implicit needs within the analysis.These findings underscore the urgent need for integrated care models that bridge service silos and promote continuity, as outlined in integrated care frameworks, which call for coordinated, person-centered approaches to address complex, interrelated needs (Citation40,Citation68). Many previous studies have identified the complex and often overlapping health needs of people experiencing homelessness, regarding both physical and mental health (Citation2–6,Citation16,Citation27,Citation28). Difficult experiences in care trajectories, often resulting in unmet service needs, have also been widely reported (Citation26,Citation29–34). However, to our knowledge, this study is the first to specifically explore the needs and experiences from the perspective of individuals with lived experience of both homelessness and ABI. By highlighting the unique challenges faced by this population at the intersection of ABI-related impairments and systemic service fragmentation, this study makes a valuable contribution to the existing literature.Several specific challenges and needs identified in this study have also been documented in previous literature on ABI. For example, difficulties with financial management and challenging behaviors have been associated with housing instability and homelessness (Citation69–72). Cognitive impairments, such as short-term memory loss, poor attention, executive dysfunction and issues with impulse control, are commonly observed in individuals with an ABI, severe mental illness, or substance use disorders. These impairments can hinder the ability to manage finances, thereby increasing the risk of housing loss (Citation12,Citation69–71). These difficulties are further exacerbated by structural barriers, such as budgeting on a limited income and navigating increasingly complex financial and banking systems (Citation69). Similarly, the quality of social interactions has been previously shown to act as a powerful homelessness risk or protective factor. Challenges with social and communication skills, such as self-expression, understanding others’ expectations, problem-solving interpersonal conflicts, and asserting oneself, have been shown to impede one’s ability to find and maintain housing (Citation73). Challenging behaviors following an ABI have also been linked to various health and social issues, as well as significantly increased use of services among adults living in the community (Citation74). Conversely, characteristics of social environments such as stigmatizing attitudes or threats to safety can negatively affect one’s ability to engage in social interactions (Citation75). These findings highlight a cyclical pattern whereby a lack of access to appropriate ABI-related services results in unmet functional needs, creating barriers to accessing and maintaining proper housing. The resulting housing instability further impedes access to the services that could support recovery and community reintegration, perpetuating a cycle of exclusion.Our findings also reflect the barriers outlined in Omerov’s review (Citation37), particularly with regards to service fragmentation, the absence of adapted support for cognitive and behavioral issues, and structural obstacles that hinder long-term recovery for individuals experiencing homelessness with an ABI. The findings highlight important interpersonal barriers, such as prejudice, which can hinder service provision. However, building strong relationships with certain community or network stakeholders can positively influence individuals’ progress and recovery. In terms of structural barriers, the physical layout of residential and care environments often fails to accommodate the cognitive and behavioral challenges faced by participants. Finally, in terms of organization, our results show that service organizations struggle to deliver timely, continuous, adequately intensive services that are adapted to individuals’ long-term, complex, comorbid health and social needs (Citation76). Participants also described avoiding health services, despite having significant needs, a phenomenon that has been well documented in prior studies (Citation39,Citation77,Citation78). Our results contribute to this body of work by demonstrating that nonuse of health services occurs even when significant symptoms of ABI are present. This suggests that, despite indicating a clear need for support, these symptoms do not necessarily lead to service engagement. These findings suggest that avoidance may result not only from individual mistrust or negative experiences, but also from the cumulative impact of repeated encounters with institutional barriers. Participants who had experienced unmet expectations or exclusion from health, social and correctional systems appeared to internalize a sense of futility toward future institutional engagement, even when services were presented as supportive or beneficial.Implications for practice and policyThis study highlights the urgent need for innovative, coordinated models of care and rehabilitation service provision to address the intersecting long-term health and social needs of individuals with ABI experiencing homelessness – needs that are unmet within fragmented service systems. Recommendations from recent studies support the implementation of such models with key components including person-centered care, multidisciplinary teams, routine screening and referral for ABI within mental health and substance use services (and vice versa), cross-sector collaboration, and a sustained, long-term approach to care (Citation49,Citation79,Citation80). Routine screening for ABI within homelessness and correctional justice settings has also shown promise as a strategy to raise awareness on the ABI-related challenges and connect individuals to specialized health care (Citation81–89). Such cross-sector collaboration could be expanded beyond mental health and substance use services to include other frequently used services for people experiencing homelessness, such as infectious disease and sexual health services. Incorporating ABI-informed practices in these settings could strengthen the early detection of ABI and coordination of care for individuals with complex, multisectoral needs.Capacity building through training service providers on ABI in these settings has also shown positive outcomes (Citation55,Citation90,Citation91). Studies report improved cross-sector knowledge of ABI and correctional justice resources (Citation90), as well as enhanced self-efficacy and capacity to address the needs of individuals with ABI in homelessness settings (Citation55,Citation91). Community-based ABI rehabilitation programs represent an effective strategy to engage individuals experiencing homelessness who tend to avoid traditional healthcare services, while addressing their multiples health and social needs. Such initiatives have been implemented in a shelter-based medical respite program (Citation79), integrated primary care settings for individuals experiencing homelessness (Citation85,Citation88) and other homelessness and domestic violence settings (Citation83,Citation84) with consistently promising outcomes. Integrating functional assessments in discharge planning from correctional or health institutions, as well as during the transition into housing may contribute to better assess and meet individuals’ functional needs (Citation92,Citation93). Finally, capacity building through initial and continued training is essential for the healthcare sector to improve its understanding of, and response to, the complex health profiles and social needs of people experiencing or at risk of homelessness (Citation50,Citation94,Citation95). These implications echo the findings of a recent realist-inspired review, which showed that innovative rehabilitation models flourish when they combine clearly defined goals and long-term funding with multidisciplinary teamwork, leadership support and system-wide outreach to bridge the gaps between fragmented services (Citation96).Strengths and limitationsThis study is innovative because, to our knowledge, it is the first to specifically explore the perspectives of homeless individuals with an ABI regarding their needs and experiences in the different services involved in their care trajectories. The large and diverse sample, regarding gender, sexual orientation and migration background, is also a notable strength. An important limitation of this study is that all participants were recruited through partner community organizations, meaning that they all had contact with at least one service point. It would have been valuable to include individuals with no service contact to compare their views with those of individuals who received at least minimal support. Additionally, as community workers identified potential participants based on their subjective knowledge of clients’ life histories rather than formal ABI documentation and diagnoses, individuals with unreported or ‘invisible’ ABIs may not have been identified or invited to participate.
