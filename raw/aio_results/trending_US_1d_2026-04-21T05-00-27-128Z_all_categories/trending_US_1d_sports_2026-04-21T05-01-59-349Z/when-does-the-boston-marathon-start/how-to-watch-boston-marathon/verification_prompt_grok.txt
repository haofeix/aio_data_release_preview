Updated April 22, 2026 01:13PMWhen I ran Boston, I never thought about what was happening behind the scenes. I was too caught up in the excitement and the magic of running. I trained for the Newton Hills, couldn’t wait to run through the Wellesley scream tunnel, dreamed about passing the Citgo sign and making the turn down Boylston Street toward the blue and yellow finish line. As a runner, you put in the hard work and train for months to run this marathon. On the other side of the finish line, 1,700 medical providers wait. They are doctors, nurses, physical therapists, athletic trainers, sports psychologists, podiatrists, medical scribes, and more. They have trained their entire careers to help you, should anything go wrong, from an ankle sprain to a major medical emergency. This year, I was on this side. Here is a glimpse from inside the medical tent of what years of planning, months of meetings, weeks of training modules, and 12 hours have taught me about running the Boston Marathon. (Photo: Kate Mihevc Edwards) Even When It’s Cold, Overheating Is Real You never know what weather Boston is going to give you come Marathon Monday. A hot year is a busy year, and up to 10% of race participants could end up in the medical tent if it is too hot. This year was a cold year; the temperature ranged from 30 degrees Fahrenheit to the low 50s, but heat injuries still occurred. Proper hydration, pacing, and picking the right race kit are all important when it comes to the weather. If your core body temperature gets too high, it gets dangerous quickly. In the medical tent, there is a section called the heat deck. If you end up there, it is because you are showing signs of severe heat illness: temperature of >104 degrees, confusion or a change in mental status, seizures, or even loss of consciousness. You may wake up in a tub of ice with five or more volunteers monitoring your vitals, holding your head, pouring cold water and ice over you while explaining what is happening, and bringing you back from the edge. Your hydration, pacing, and clothing should all be an important part of your race plan. While you can’t predict the weather, you can be flexible and adjust your race day strategy to match the environment so you don’t end up in the ice tub. It’s Not the Hills: General Health Scares Bring Most to the Med Tent Some of the major things we see in the medical tent—from cardiovascular incidents to heat illness—are because athletes race when they are sick, not wanting to miss the race. Or, in the heat of the race day moment, they forget to take their medicine. But just because you’re capable of running a BQ doesn’t mean you’re exempt from the rules of good health. Another mistake? Popping OTC pain pills before heading to the start line. Ibuprofen is a common drug runners take when they are hurting, but taking it when running long distances, such as a marathon, can put you at a higher risk of GI issues, acute kidney injury, or even heart issues, so don’t take it before you run. Also, just because you are in shape doesn’t always mean you are healthy. If you started running to stop drinking, to lose weight or to manage stress that is great. You still need to make sure your body is ready to run 26.2 miles from a physical and physiological standpoint. You can make it through training only to have an issue totally derail your race, so don’t skip your yearly physical. If you don’t have a primary care doctor, get one. It is so much better to prevent something than it is to end up wishing you had. You Can’t Always Outrun Genetics It is rare, but there are some genetic heart conditions that may show up in a major running event like a marathon, even if you have never had an issue before. At Boston, the team is prepared for this. (We did see some cardiac issues and crazy ECGs, which is typical.) There is a field ICU set up, and there are world-class cardiologists, ER nurses, and physicians ready. I unfortunately have a rare genetic heart disease and passed out in not one, but three major marathons before I found out what was truly wrong—I nearly died because I ignored the signs my body was sending me. If you have ever noticed any of these symptoms during or after exercise: heart racing; fatigue that doesn’t match your effort or training; tightness in your chest; around your sports bra, neck, or shoulder; or shortness of breath or dizziness during or right after exercise, don’t just ignore it or push through. Get it checked out. Behind the scenes: These are the medical personnel on call for runners. (Photo: Kate Mihevc Edwards) Chicken Broth for the Runner’s Soul Cramps Cramping can set in while running or right after you stop. Even though you may want a deep tissue massage or some stretching when you begin to cramp, it is not likely what you need on race day. Cramping on race day is typically a result of an electrolyte imbalance. If you are a heavy sweater and notice there is a lot of salt on your face or body, you probably have lost too much sodium. If you come into the medical tent looking for a massage and we hand you some chicken broth, don’t be surprised or disappointed. I promise you, chicken broth can be like magic when you are cramping. Running is fun, but ending up in the medical tent is not. Even though the providers waiting for you are lovely people (I promise we are!), we don’t want to see you either. While you can’t predict if you are going to step into a pothole, you can be flexible when you feel off or the weather is not ideal. Adjusting your race plan to how your body feels and your environment can make all the difference. Also, please remember that even though you are in shape, running doesn’t make genetics or past medical history go away completely. You can prepare your body by getting checkups regularly, setting reminders to take your medicine, avoiding ibuprofen before running long distances, and having a proper hydration plan.
