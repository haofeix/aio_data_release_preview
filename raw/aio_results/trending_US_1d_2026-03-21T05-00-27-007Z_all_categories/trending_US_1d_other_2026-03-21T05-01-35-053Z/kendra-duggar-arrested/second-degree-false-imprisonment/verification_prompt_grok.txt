Misdemeanor vs. Felony False Imprisonment False imprisonment is known as a “wobbler” offense under California law. This means that it can be charged as either a misdemeanor or a felony offense. Sec. 14-18. False imprisonment in the second degree. (a) A person commits false imprisonment in the second degree if he knowingly restrains another person without legal authority. (b) In any prosecution under this section, it shall be an affirmative defense that the person restrained: (1) Was on or in the immediate vicinity of the premises of a retail mercantile establishment and he was restrained for the purpose of investigation or questioning as to the ownership of any merchandise; and (2) Was restrained in a reasonable manner and for not more than a reasonable time; and (3) Was restrained to permit such investigation or questioning by a police officer, or by the owner of the mercantile establishment, his authorized employee or agent; and (4) That such police officer, owner, employee or agent had reasonable grounds to believe that the person so detained was committing or attempting to commit theft of merchandise on the premises; Provided nothing in this section shall prohibit or restrict any person restrained pursuant to this section from maintaining any applicable civil remedy if no theft has occurred. (c) False imprisonment in the second degree is an offense. (d) As used in this section, unless the context otherwise requires: (1) Restrain shall mean to restrict a person's movement in such a manner as to interfere substantially with his liberty: a. By means of force, threat, or deception; or b. """
False imprisonment occurs when one person intentionally restrains another in a way that confines the individual within a bounded area without consent or legal authority. It is recognized both as a crime and as an intentional tort.Prima Facie CaseTo establish a prima facie case, the plaintiff must show:The defendant acted willfullyThe defendant intended to confine the plaintiff without consent and without lawful authorityThe defendant’s act caused the plaintiff’s confinementThe plaintiff was aware of the confinementBounded AreaRestraint may be imposed by physical barriers (such as a locked door), physical force, failure to release, or misuse of legal authority. An area is bounded only if the plaintiff’s movement is restricted in all directions. A reasonable means of safe escape prevents an area from being considered bounded. If escape would cause risk of physical harm, or if threats are made against the plaintiff or family members, the area is deemed bounded.Threats of False ImprisonmentThreats of immediate physical force can constitute restraint. Mere threats of future imprisonment are insufficient. Courts consider whether the plaintiff had a reasonable fear of injury.Invalid Use of Legal AuthorityFalse imprisonment may occur if a person is detained under an invalid warrant, without a warrant, or under a warrant executed unlawfully. The duration of detention is immaterial if personal liberty is restrained. See Schenck v. Pro-Choice Network, 519 U.S. 357 (1997).Shopkeeper’s PrivilegeA shopkeeper who reasonably believes a customer is committing theft may detain the customer for a reasonable time and in a reasonable manner to investigate. This affirmative defense is known as shopkeeper’s privilege.Related TortsFalse imprisonment is related to two other intentional torts: malicious prosecution and abuse of process.Malicious prosecution requires proof that the defendant acted without probable cause, acted with malice, caused the prosecution to proceed, and that the plaintiff was not guilty of the alleged misconduct.Abuse of process requires proof that the defendant misused the legal process to extort, harass, or threaten the plaintiff.[Last reviewed in August of 2025 by the Wex Definitions Team] Keywordstort law criminal law intentional tortWexLIFE EVENTS accidents & injuries (tort law) standards of tort liability tort damages THE LEGAL PROCESS courts criminal law criminal procedure wex definitions courts and procedure criminal law and procedure

A misdemeanor charge may sound minor compared to a felony, but the consequences of misdemeanors can reach far beyond the courtroom. In Florida, even a first-time misdemeanor—whether classified as a first-degree offense (punishable by up to a year in jail) or a second-degree offense (up to 60 days)—can set off a chain reaction that affects almost every aspect of your life. The true impact isn’t limited to fines or brief jail time; it can follow you into job interviews, apartment applications, and important personal milestones. If you’re in Sarasota, Manatee, or DeSoto County and facing a misdemeanor, understanding both the immediate legal penalties and the less obvious, long-term effects is vital. This guide breaks down the seven most significant consequences of a misdemeanor conviction, from employment and housing challenges to professional licensing issues and public benefits. You’ll also find clear explanations of Florida’s court process, options for sealing or expunging your record, and practical steps for minimizing the fallout. With the right information and support, it’s possible to make confident decisions and protect your future. Read on for straightforward guidance, actionable advice, and direct links to trusted resources—so you can move forward with clarity and peace of mind. What Is a Misdemeanor Under Florida Law? In Florida, misdemeanors are criminal offenses that carry lighter penalties than felonies but are more serious than infractions. Florida Statute 775.082 divides misdemeanors into two categories—first-degree and second-degree—each with its own maximum jail term and fine range. While misdemeanors may not result in state prison sentences, they can still trigger jail time, hefty fines, and lasting repercussions on your record. Below is a summary of the statutory penalties for misdemeanors under Florida law: Degree Maximum Jail Time Maximum Fine First-Degree Up to 1 year in county jail Up to $1,000 Second-Degree Up to 60 days in county jail Up to $500 Misdemeanors differ from more minor infractions—such as traffic tickets or ordinance violations—which typically carry only civil fines and no jail time. At the same time, they fall short of felonies, which can lead to state prison sentences, larger fines, and enhanced collateral consequences like loss of voting rights or permanent firearm prohibitions. Definition and Degrees of Misdemeanors Florida Statute 775.082 defines a misdemeanor as any criminal offense for which the penal code does not specify a felony punishment. The two degrees are: First-Degree Misdemeanor Punishable by up to 1 year in county jail and a fine of up to $1,000. Certain offenses—like Driving Under the Influence (DUI) or domestic battery—may carry mandatory minimum jail terms or additional court assessments. Second-Degree Misdemeanor Carrying up to 60 days in county jail and a fine of up to $500. Many minor offenses, such as simple possession of marijuana (under a specific threshold) or trespassing, fall into this category. Judges may also impose alternative sentences—like probation, community service, or day fines—in lieu of or in addition to incarceration. Common Examples of Misdemeanor Offenses Below are some of the more frequent misdemeanor charges in Florida, along with a snapshot of typical penalties: Driving Under the Influence (DUI) First-degree misdemeanor; up to 1 year in jail and fines starting around $500, plus license suspension. Simple Battery First- or second-degree (depending on injury); up to 1 year in jail for first-degree or 60 days for second-degree, plus up to $1,000 in fines. Petty Theft Typically second-degree if value is under $100; up to 60 days in jail and up to $500 in fines. Criminal Mischief (Property Damage) Second-degree for damage under $1,000; penalties include up to 60 days in jail and fines up to $500. Each case turns on its own facts—such as prior convictions, the value of loss, or presence of aggravating factors—so these ranges can shift upward when mandatory minimums or enhancements apply. How Misdemeanor Convictions Are Treated in Florida Courts and Collateral Consequences When a misdemeanor charge makes its way into a Florida courtroom, there’s a fairly structured process designed to balance efficiency with your right to due process. It generally begins with an arraignment, where formal charges are announced and you enter a plea. Next comes discovery, during which both sides exchange evidence, followed by any plea negotiations to potentially resolve the case without a full trial. If no agreement is reached, the matter proceeds to trial, where a judge—or sometimes a jury—determines guilt and imposes a sentence. Judges rely on statutory guidelines and a sentencing score sheet to calculate appropriate penalties. Florida’s Criminal Punishment Code assigns points based on factors such as the severity of the offense, your prior record, and any aggravating or mitigating circumstances. This framework gives judges a starting point for fines, jail time, and probation. However, with misdemeanors, there’s often flexibility to tailor sentences—perhaps substituting a short jail stay with community service or probation—when circumstances warrant. Financial Penalties: Fines and Court Costs Beyond the headline fine set by statute (up to $1,000 for a first-degree misdemeanor, up to $500 for a second-degree), Florida law mandates a series of assessment fees that fund court operations and victims’ services. Typical components include: Court costs: Flat fees imposed by the clerk to cover administrative expenses. State assessments: Surcharges that support county law libraries, crime labs, criminal conflict and civil regional counsel. Victims’ Compensation Fund contributions: Mandatory payments that help reimburse victims for medical bills, lost wages, and counseling. Public defender or prosecutor fees: In cases where representation costs are shifted to the defendant. These add-ons can double or even triple the base fine. Falling behind on payments can trigger additional penalties, such as interest, late fees, or even civil judgments. If you’re facing financial hardship, you can ask the court for a payment plan or a waiver of certain costs, but you’ll need to demonstrate genuine inability to pay. Jail Time and Probation Terms Misdemeanor convictions carry maximum jail terms of 1 year (first-degree) or 60 days (second-degree), but judges often suspend these sentences in favor of more constructive alternatives. Common options include: Split sentences: Serving a short stint in county jail followed by probation. Straight probation: Supervision for up to one year, during which you must comply with conditions like community service, counseling, random drug testing, or restitution. House arrest or electronic monitoring: Allowing you to remain at home under GPS or ankle-bracelet supervision. Probation is more than a courtesy—it’s a court-ordered contract. Violating any term can lead to a probation-violation hearing and reinstatement of the original jail sentence. Firearm Ownership Restrictions After a Misdemeanor A misdemeanor conviction can do more than ding your record—it can strip away your right to keep or bear arms. Federal law, through 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(9), bars anyone convicted of a misdemeanor crime of domestic violence from possessing firearms. In Florida, state courts follow suit, enforcing both federal prohibitions and their own restrictions when certain misdemeanors are involved. Knowing which offenses trigger a loss of gun rights, and how to regain them, is essential for anyone hoping to lawfully own or use a firearm after a conviction. Federal Domestic Violence Misdemeanor Firearm Ban Under 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(9), the moment you’re convicted of a misdemeanor domestic violence offense—defined as the intentional use or attempted use of physical force against an intimate partner—you fall into a lifetime ban on firearm possession. This prohibition applies regardless of whether the conviction occurred in state or federal court. Violating the ban can lead to felony charges, hefty fines, and up to 10 years in prison. Even if your case didn’t involve children, the statute’s broad language ensnares most intimate-partner battery convictions, leaving no room for judicial discretion once the conviction is final. Other Misdemeanor Offenses Triggering Gun Restrictions While the domestic-violence ban is the most sweeping, other misdemeanors can also put you in federal or state crosshairs: Brandishing or pointing a firearm (often a second-degree misdemeanor) can trigger a temporary prohibition until the conviction is cleared. Stalking or harassment charges, when classified as misdemeanors, may fall under 18 U.S.C. § 921(a)(33) and lead to a firearms disability; for guidance on harassment defenses, see what happens when you get charged with harassment. Certain drug-possession or paraphernalia convictions can invoke a three- to five-year ban under federal law, depending on the circumstances. Florida law mirrors these federal rules and adds its own layer: for example, convictions involving controlled substances—even minor amounts of marijuana—can result in a state-level prohibition on purchasing or carrying firearms until all terms of probation or supervision are satisfied. Pathways to Restoring Firearm Rights Restoring gun rights after a misdemeanor ban is challenging but not impossible: Expungement or sealing – Although sealing or expunging a record in Florida (FDLE Certificate of Eligibility) can remove the conviction from most background checks, it does not automatically lift a federal firearms ban. Petition for relief in federal court – Under 18 U.S.C. § 925(c), individuals subject to the domestic-violence ban can apply to the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives (ATF) for relief. Success hinges on demonstrating rehabilitation, a substantial passage of time, and that restoration is unlikely to harm public safety. State-level relief – Florida courts may restore civil rights—including firearm rights—in connection with executive clemency or through the Department of Agriculture and Consumer Services for concealed-weapons permits. However, Florida cannot override federal prohibitions. Because the interplay between federal and state rules is complex, many turn to resources like The Defense Firm’s guide to navigate relief applications and gather the supporting evidence needed to make a compelling case. By understanding these restrictions and exploring every available avenue for relief, you’ll be better positioned to protect your constitutional rights and move forward after a misdemeanor conviction. How to Seal or Expunge Your Record in Florida If you’ve been convicted of a misdemeanor in Florida and completed all court-ordered obligations, you may be eligible to clear your record through sealing or expungement. Both options limit public access to your criminal history, but they work in different ways and have distinct eligibility requirements. Below is an overview of each process and a step-by-step guide to obtaining a Certificate of Eligibility and petitioning the court. Difference Between Sealing and Expungement While sealing and expungement are often mentioned together, they serve separate purposes: Sealing a record makes it confidential. Government agencies and certain employers may still access sealed files, but the public—landlords, most private companies, and online background-check services—cannot. Expungement actually destroys the official record, so it no longer appears on any state or local database. Once expunged, you can truthfully say you do not have a record for the covered offense. Choosing between the two depends on your goals: expungement offers the most thorough relief, while sealing may be simpler if you only need to block general public access. Eligibility Criteria for Misdemeanors Not every conviction qualifies for sealing or expungement. Under Florida law, you must meet these key requirements: Completion of all court obligations Probation, payment of fines, restitution, and any drug or anger-management programs must be fully satisfied. No more than one misdemeanor conviction A single nonviolent misdemeanor is generally allowed; multiple misdemeanors or any felony convictions disqualify you. No prior sealing or expungement You cannot re-seal or re-expunge the same offense. Waiting period A six-month waiting period starts once you complete probation or any court-ordered supervision. Always verify your specific offense on the FDLE’s eligibility list; some misdemeanors involving domestic violence or sexual misconduct are never eligible. Application Process and Timeline Request a Certificate of Eligibility (COE) Visit the FDLE’s Certificate of Eligibility Instructions. Complete the FDLE application form, submit fingerprint cards, and pay the $75 fee. FDLE reviews your history (including local dispositions) and issues a COE if you qualify. Expect a processing time of roughly 12 weeks. File a Petition in Court Once you receive the COE, draft a petition to seal or expunge your record in the county where you were convicted. Attach your COE, court disposition, and any supporting affidavits. Pay the court’s filing fee (varies by county). Serve the State Attorney and Law Enforcement Provide copies of your petition to the State Attorney’s office and local law-enforcement agency. They have an opportunity to object—often they will not if you meet all statutory requirements. Attend a Hearing (if required) In most misdemeanor cases, the court grants relief without a hearing. If a hearing is set, be prepared to briefly explain compliance with all terms and the public-interest benefits of sealing or expunging your record. Final Order When the judge signs the order, the clerk sends it to FDLE, local courts, and the arresting agency. Sealed records become confidential immediately; expunged files are purged from all state and local indexes. With a sealed or expunged misdemeanor, you can legally deny the conviction on most job, housing, and credit applications—giving you a clear path forward.
