The core stage then drops away.At that point, the upper stage, with Orion on top, will be coasting upward. At nearly 1,381 miles above Earth’s surface, the engine in the upper stage will fire for the first time to ensure the vehicle stays in orbit and does not re-enter the atmosphere.This will surpass the record for the farthest that people have traveled from Earth while still in orbit around the planet.(That record was once held by the astronauts of Gemini XI, who in 1966 traveled to 853 miles above Earth’s surface. Then in 2024, Jared Isaacman, before he became NASA administrator, eclipsed that record when he led a private space mission called Polaris Dawn, to a height of 870 miles.)Then comes unusual part of the Artemis II trajectory.Artemis II’s 1,381-mile record will last only about 13 hours. As the Orion capsule swoops past Earth along its elliptical orbit, the second stage will fire again to put the spacecraft in what NASA calls a high-Earth orbit. The second stage will then be discarded.And by “high,” NASA means “really, really high.”On the second orbit around Earth, Artemis II will set a brand-new record: 43,730 miles, or close to one-fifth of the distance to the moon. (That distance, compared to Mr. Isaacman’s current record, would be like breaking the men’s world record for the shot put of 77 feet 3.5 inches by flinging the 16-pound metal ball out of the stadium, three-quarters of a mile.)The discarded stage will serve one more role, as the target to practice maneuvers mimicking docking during the first busy day in space. On Artemis missions planned for 2027 and 2028, Orion will have to dock with a moon lander that will be launched separately.This wide, looping path, lasting about 24 hours, provides time for checking out the key systems aboard Orion, including life support and deep-space communications.“We could bring them, our astronauts, back home, if we don’t like what we see,” Mr. Isaacman said in a recent interview.NASA never did anything like this for the Apollo missions. During those journeys to the moon, the Saturn V rocket lifted the astronauts to low-Earth orbit. Then the third stage of the Saturn V, still connected to the spacecraft where the astronauts sat, fired a second time, pushing them toward the moon — a maneuver known as translunar injection.The agency did something similar during Artemis I in 2022, and future Artemis missions may follow a similar course.But with Artemis II, the S.L.S. upper stage will be discarded first.For a typical mission trajectory, that would leave Orion stranded around Earth.“Orion, on its own, doesn’t have the capability to do a translunar injection burn from low-Earth orbit,” said Natasha Peake, a flight dynamics officer working on the mission.But the high-Earth orbit provides most of the energy needed to reach the moon.As Orion accelerates around Earth the second time, its engines will fire to provide a final outward nudge.From there, except for some course corrections, the spacecraft is already on its path back to Earth. But the steps for doing so can be broken down into four stages: Chilldown: This initial stage cools the fuel lines down in order to reduce thermal shock across them. As we've already seen, it's the quickest phase.Slow fill: This slowly loads Artemis II's extremely cold liquid hydrogen fuel into the rocket, doing so gradually to condition the rocket's plumbing and tanks from room temperature to the -253°C (-423.4°F) temperature the liquid hydrogen is kept at. More on that in a minute.Fast fill: Once temperatures inside the tank stabilize, the system moves to fast fill, pumping 8,000 gallons of liquid hydrogen and 1,300 gallons of liquid oxygen into the rocket's tanks each minute.Topping/replenish: Once all the propellant is inside the rocket, engineers closely monitor the rocket for leaks while slowly topping it up to account for some of the fuel boiling off into gas. April 1, 2026 at 12:55 PM Filling speeds April 1, 2026 at 1:02 PM Why use hydrogen fuel? That's because as the smallest molecule, hydrogen can readily pass through solid metals such as steel, nickel and titanium by diffusing through their atomic lattices.And with a boiling point of minus 423 degrees Fahrenheit (minus 253 Celsius, or 20 kelvins), it must be stored at extremely cold temperatures, which can easily freeze seals beyond safe limits.Despite these drawbacks, hydrogen still provides the highest specific impulse of any chemical propellant, and also burns without producing any harmful byproducts, making it NASA's fuel of choice since the Apollo program. April 1, 2026 at 5:40 PM What traditions do astronauts have for the day of the launch? One of the main traditions happens long before the walkout photos. In the morning before a launch, crewmembers typically sit down and eat a classic steak-and-egg breakfast to start the day — a ritual tied to the early days of American spaceflight. NASA astronaut Alan Shepard is believed to have started this tradition in 1961, before the launch of NASA's Mercury-Redstone 3 (MR-3) mission, a suborbital flight that lasted 15 minutes and 28 seconds.After eating, crewmembers participate in another tradition, a prelaunch card game. The game, usually a form of poker or blackjack, plays out until the commander of the mission loses. It's a bit of superstition meant to "use up" the bad luck before anyone heads out to the launchpad. (Image credit: NASA) As NASA continues to stream live coverage of the upcoming Artemis II launch, viewers saw the four astronauts getting suited up in their orange spacesuits. Each suit has been custom fitted for each astronaut to ensure the most comfortable fit.
