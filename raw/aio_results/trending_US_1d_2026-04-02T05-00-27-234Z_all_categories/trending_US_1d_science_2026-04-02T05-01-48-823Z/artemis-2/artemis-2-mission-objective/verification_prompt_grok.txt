It was the first crewed flight of the NASA-led Artemis program and the first crewed flight beyond low Earth orbit since Apollo 17 in 1972. Artemis II was the second flight of the Space Launch System (SLS) and the first crewed flight of the Orion spacecraft, named Integrity by the four-person crew. The mission was a test flight supporting subsequent Artemis missions aimed at returning humans to the lunar surface. Originally designated Exploration Mission-2 (EM-2) and intended to support the canceled Asteroid Redirect Mission, its objectives were revised after the establishment of the Artemis program in 2017. The mission's primary goal was to validate the Orion spacecraft's systems, crew operations, and mission procedures ahead of sustained lunar exploration in future Artemis missions.[10] Artemis II's mission objectives were similar to those of Apollo 8 in 1968, the first crewed lunar flight of the Apollo program, while its free-return trajectory resembled that flown by Apollo 13 in 1970. Victor Glover (an African-American) became the first person of color, Christina Koch the first woman, Canadian Space Agency astronaut Jeremy Hansen the first non-U.S. citizen, and commander Reid Wiseman the oldest person to travel beyond low Earth orbit and around the Moon. During their lunar flyby, the crew set the record for human distance from Earth, 252,756 miles (406,771 km), breaking Apollo 13's record of 248,655 miles (400,171 km). After launch, Artemis II gained widespread global attention for its achievements and inclusive crew, giving rise to the term Moon joy. History Mission planning and launch date selection (2017–2021) In 2017, Exploration Mission-2 was planned as a single-launch flight of a Space Launch System (SLS) Block 1B rocket equipped with the Exploration Upper Stage, carrying a lunar Block 1 Orion spacecraft, and a payload capacity of 50.7 tonnes (112,000 lb). The mission concept involved rendezvousing with an asteroid that would have been placed in lunar orbit by the robotic Asteroid Redirect Mission, allowing astronauts to conduct spacewalks and collect samples.[11][12] After the Asteroid Redirect Mission was cancelled in April 2017,[13] NASA proposed an alternative mission involving an eight‑day free-return trajectory around the Moon with a crew of four astronauts.[14] Another 2017 proposal suggested sending four astronauts aboard Orion on an 8‑ to 21‑day lunar mission to deliver the first element of the planned Lunar Gateway space station.[15] This proposal did not come to fruition, and in March 2018, NASA decided that the Gateway's initial module would instead launch on a commercial rocket[16] due to delays in constructing the Mobile Launcher required for the more powerful Exploration Upper Stage.[17] The SpaceX Falcon Heavy was selected as the launch vehicle.[18] However, the Lunar Gateway program was cancelled in March 2026.[19] Hardware development, testing and integration (2021–2025) SLS core stage for Artemis II lifted into High Bay 2 of the Vehicle Assembly Building shortly after stacking operations began in December 2024 On February 11, 2023, NASA rotated the Artemis II core stage's engine section to a horizontal position, marking the final major milestone before integration with the rest of the vehicle. Rise, a zero-gravity indicator plush toy onboard Artemis II with the astronauts, was designed by 8-year-old Lucas Ye.[73][74] Mission Diagram showing the planned objectives of the Artemis II mission Artemis II's mission was a crewed flight test with four astronauts evaluating the performance of the Space Launch System (SLS) rocket along with the Orion spacecraft and its European Service Module (ESM) in deep space. The first day of the mission was spent largely in high Earth orbit, where the crew conducted system checkouts. Orion operated in a highly elliptical, high Earth orbit with a period of roughly 24 hours, allowing extended testing of onboard systems. During this phase, the crew evaluated life support and other critical spacecraft systems, and performed a rendezvous and proximity operations demonstration using the spent Interim Cryogenic Propulsion Stage (ICPS) as a target. After NASA mission managers confirmed Orion's performance, the spacecraft executed a trans-lunar injection (TLI) burn to depart Earth orbit. Orion then traveled toward the Moon on a free-return trajectory, looping around the far side before naturally returning toward Earth on a free-return trajectory without requiring additional propulsion for the return leg.[75][76] Notably, the crew received "wake-up calls" from Mission Control on every day of the flight, a NASA tradition since the Apollo missions, consisting of music designed to keep the crew on a steady rhythm and boost morale and inspirational speeches recorded specifically for the flight, including messages from Apollo astronauts Charlie Duke and Jim Lovell.[77][78] Artemis II's trajectory can be divided into several key phases, over an approximately ten-day trip:[79][80] Launch Key events from launch and ascent to space Artemis II lifts off from Launch Complex 39B at NASA's Kennedy Space Center in Florida on April 1, 2026 The crew arrived at Kennedy Space Center on March 27,[81] and the launch countdown began on March 30.[82] The mission launched aboard an SLS rocket on April 1 from Kennedy Space Center's Launch Complex 39B at 22:35:12 UTC (6:35:12 p.m. EDT, local time at the launch site).[5][83] It was the first crewed launch from LC-39B since STS-116 in 2006. The four RS-25 main engines on the core stage ignited approximately seven seconds before liftoff; after their performance was confirmed at full power, the solid rocket boosters, whose ignition cannot be reversed, ignited at T-0, providing the majority of thrust during the first two minutes of flight. Booster separation occurred at roughly 3,100 miles per hour (5,000 km/h) at an altitude of 30 miles (48 km). The boosters subsequently splashed down in the Atlantic Ocean about six minutes after launch. Unlike the Space Shuttle boosters, the SLS boosters were not recovered.[84][85] Wiseman monitored the launch from the left seat of Orion at the primary controls. The flight was fully automated, and no crew intervention was required, though Wiseman would have been able to issue an abort command if necessary. The core stage burned for about eight minutes before separation, leaving Orion in a highly elliptical orbit with an apogee of roughly 1,400 miles (2,300 km), nearly five times higher than the International Space Station, but a suborbital perigee. The ICPS upper stage did not fire during the initial ascent.[84] The core stage made a destructive reentry over the Pacific Ocean approximately two hours after launch.[85] High Earth orbit and systems checkout The launch abort system after it was jettisoned, as viewed by an automatic camera on Integrity Immediately after main-engine cutoff, Koch and Hansen unstrapped from their seats to set up and test essential life support systems aboard the spacecraft, including the water dispenser, firefighting masks, and toilet. All systems checked out (after the crew resolved minor issues with the toilet and water dispenser),[86][87] giving mission managers the confidence to proceed with the ICPS burn at apogee, about 50 minutes after liftoff, to raise Orion's perigee.[86][88] Prior to this burn, Orion's perigee was suborbital, a deliberate safety measure that would have ensured a natural reentry in the event of a major anomaly. The ICPS burn raised the perigee out of the atmosphere, placing the spacecraft into a stable low Earth orbit.[84][85] When the spacecraft reached this new perigee about an hour later, it executed a 15-minute burn to raise its next apogee to 44,000 miles (71,000 km; 38,000 nmi), establishing a 23.5-hour high Earth orbit.[84] This marked the first time a crewed spacecraft had entered a high Earth orbit without proceeding directly to the Moon.[89] After this burn, which consumed nearly all the fuel in the ICPS, the Orion and ESM separated from the upper stage. The crew then conducted a "proximity operations" demonstration using the ICPS as a target. Over approximately 70 minutes, Glover, now in the left seat of Orion, took manual control of the spacecraft and performed a series of maneuvers to evaluate handling qualities and practice techniques for future docking operations. The ICPS was equipped with a docking target, enabling tests of Orion's ability to manually maneuver relative to another spacecraft using onboard navigation sensors and reaction control thrusters.[84][90][91] Following the demonstration, Orion returned to automated control while the ICPS performed a deorbit burn for destructive reentry over the Pacific Ocean,[84][90] deploying its rideshare CubeSats during this phase.[85] After these operations, the crew reconfigured the cabin for spaceflight, set up their flywheel exercise device and used it to conduct a life-support system stress test through physical activity, performing both aerobic and resistance exercises using a compact cable-driven system designed to operate within the spacecraft's mass and volume constraints, and had dinner.[84][92] The first sleep period was divided into two four-hour segments, interrupted to monitor a 43-second burn by the European Service Module that raised the spacecraft's perigee again to prepare for a trans-lunar injection (TLI) burn.[93] Following the burn, the astronauts returned to sleep while NASA managers reviewed spacecraft performance before authorizing the TLI burn.[84] Trans-lunar injection and outbound flight Hello, World photograph taken by Wiseman from Integrity after trans-lunar injection, showing the full Earth with its night side illuminated by the Moon. On flight day 2, following completion of high Earth orbit operations and system checks, Orion performed a 5-minute, 49-second TLI burn using the ESM's AJ10 main engine. This was the only use of the main engine during the mission; subsequent maneuvers were carried out by the eight smaller R-4D secondary engines.[94] The burn consumed approximately 1,000 pounds (450 kg) of hypergolic propellant and placed the spacecraft on a free-return trajectory around the Moon, requiring only minor course corrections for the remainder of the mission.[95] On flight day 3, the first of three planned outbound trajectory correction burns was deemed unnecessary after Mission Control determined the spacecraft was already on a favorable trajectory.[96] The crew encountered an issue with the built-in space toilet when urine froze in the vent lines, preventing it from being ejected into space. This was resolved by switching on vent heaters and by rotating the spacecraft to expose the vent to the warmth of the sun, melting the urine.[87] On flight day 4, Koch and Hansen took turns manually controlling the spacecraft to evaluate its performance in deep space. Over 41 minutes, they tested two thruster control modes (six degrees of freedom and three degrees of freedom) to provide engineers with further data and perspectives on the spacecraft's handling qualities.[97] On flight day 5, Orion performed a 17+1⁄2-second outbound trajectory correction burn to refine its path to the Moon.[98] Of the three planned outbound correction burns, this was the only one executed. The crew also tested their Orion Crew Survival System suits and conferred with mission control to review lunar surface targets for observation and photography during the flyby and finalize observation techniques.[99] Lunar flyby Integrity approaching the Moon on April 6 On flight day 6, Orion entered the Moon's sphere of influence, where lunar gravity became the dominant force shaping its trajectory.[98] Orion flew around the Moon at a closest approach of about 4,067 miles (6,545 km; 3,534 nmi) from the far-side lunar surface at 23:00 UTC on April 6. Its farthest distance from Earth was 252,756 miles (406,771 km; 219,639 nmi) at 23:02 UTC, and it surpassed Apollo 13's 248,655 miles (400,171 km; 216,075 nmi) record as the farthest crewed mission from Earth at 17:56 UTC.[9][78][100][101] As the crew passed behind the Moon, Orion experienced a planned loss of signal for 40 minutes, beginning at 22:46 UTC, and recovered signals from Earth at 23:24 UTC.[102] During their flyby, the crew observed two unnamed craters which they proposed be named Integrity, after their spacecraft, and Carroll, after Wiseman's late wife, who died of cancer in 2020.[103] The crew photographed a solar eclipse, with the Moon blocking out the Sun. After the blackout, the Orion experienced a solar eclipse. It lasted for 57 minutes, beginning at 01:35 UTC and lasting until 02:32 UTC. The crew donned eclipse glasses until the Sun was fully obscured, after which they observed both the solar corona as well as "impact flashes" from meteoroids impacting the dark portion of the Moon. Stars and planets, including Venus, Mars, Saturn and Mercury, were visible alongside the corona, as was earthshine illuminating the Moon.[104][105][106] Return flight On flight day 7, Orion and its crew exited the Moon's sphere of influence and began their return to Earth on a free-return trajectory.[107][108] The crew conducted a 15-minute audio-only call with NASA astronauts Jessica Meir, Jack Hathaway, and Chris Williams, and ESA astronaut Sophie Adenot aboard the International Space Station,[109] pointing out that together they became the humans furthest ever from another.[110] Afterwards, they debriefed with NASA science officers on their lunar observations while memories of the flyby were still fresh. The remainder of the day included staggered off-duty periods for rest ahead of final return preparations.[111] The day concluded with a 15-second trajectory correction burn, the first of three that refined the path of the spacecraft toward Earth.[8][112] On flight day 8, a planned manual control test by Wiseman and Glover was canceled so controllers could conduct additional testing of Orion's propulsion system. The change allowed engineers to gather data on a small helium leak in the ESM and better characterize its behavior in flight. Although the leak did not affect mission safety, the data is expected to inform propulsion system modifications for future Artemis missions. A planned demonstration of the spacecraft's radiation shielding was also canceled.[113][94] On flight day 9, Orion performed a 9-second trajectory correction burn to refine its return path to Earth. The remainder of the day was largely devoted to cabin preparations, with the crew stowing equipment, installing their seats, and reviewing reentry procedures ahead of splashdown.[114] On flight day 10, Orion conducted an 8-second trajectory correction burn, the final firing of the ESM before it was jettisoned prior to reentry.[112] NASA said that the mission had consumed less than half of the fuel aboard the ESM.[94] After the separation, the thrusters on the crew module fired for 19 seconds to push it away from the ESM and put it into the proper angle for reentry.[115] Re-entry and splashdown Landing path of Artemis II On flight day 10, Integrity re-entered Earth's atmosphere at a maximum velocity of about 24,664 miles per hour (36,174 ft/s; 39,693 km/h; 11 km/s) before decelerating.[116][2] This maximum velocity, while higher than generally seen for missions to low Earth orbit, is comparable to those reached during lunar-return missions of the Apollo program.[117] Mission planners had originally scheduled a skip reentry profile, in which the Orion spacecraft would briefly dip into the upper atmosphere to generate lift, dissipate energy, and improve landing precision. However, this was replaced with a steeper direct entry trajectory to limit heating duration following the unexpected heat shield erosion observed after Artemis I's reentry.[118][119] Integrity about to splashdown in the Pacific Ocean Splashdown occurred April 11, 2026, 00:07:27 UTC (April 10, 5:07:27 p.m. PDT local time), in the Pacific Ocean southwest of San Diego, California,[1][120] where the U.S. Navy recovered the crew.[121][122] It became the first crewed NASA mission to be recovered by the Navy since the Apollo–Soyuz Test Project in 1975.[123] NASA confirmed on April 7, 2026 (flight day 7), that the amphibious transport dock USS John P. Murtha had departed Naval Base San Diego for the recovery area.[8] Navy divers helping the crew onto the "front porch" Following splashdown, NASA reported a communications issue between Orion and recovery teams, preventing them from approaching the spacecraft. When communications were established, the joint NASA and Navy team approached the capsule in inflatable boats. Navy divers stabilized the spacecraft using a sea anchor and attached an inflatable collar to balance the spacecraft. They then attached an inflatable raft, known as a "front porch", to the side hatch, and conducted initial checks before crew egress.[124][125] Crew members exited the capsule individually onto the raft, where they were fitted with hoisting gear and lifted into helicopters. The helicopters transported the astronauts to the USS John P. Murtha, where they were taken to the medical bay for post-flight evaluation.[124] Post-landing testing included functional assessments such as obstacle course activities and a simulated spacewalk to evaluate readaptation to gravity following the mission and in preparation for future lunar and Mars operations.[121] After crew recovery, divers attached a winch line to the capsule and secured additional tending lines. The spacecraft was drawn into a cradle within the ship's well deck, which can be partially flooded to allow the capsule to be brought aboard in the water. The well deck was then drained to secure Orion aboard. Orion was later transported to Naval Base San Diego before transfer to Kennedy Space Center for inspection, data retrieval, refurbishment, and post-flight processing.[124] Aerial and airborne support included a modified NASA Martin RB-57F Canberra (N926NA), which provided high-altitude video of the landing and recovery,[126] and a Cessna 208 Caravan (N97826), which transmitted live aerial views of the splashdown site.[127][128] Navy Sikorsky MH-60S Seahawks helicopters supported imaging and recovery operations.[129] Experiments The inside of Integrity and the crew working during flight Deep space technology for humans The mission included a payload titled AVATAR (A Virtual Astronaut Tissue Analog Response) which can mimic individual astronaut organs, marking the first time that AVATAR has been tested outside of the International Space Station and Earth's Van Allen belt.[130][131] Crew health for this mission was critical for missions in deep space in the future. The mission also included a new payload titled ARCHeR (Artemis Research for Crew Health & Readiness). For ARCHeR, crew members wore movement and sleep monitors before, during, and after the mission to study real-time health and behavioral information for crew members so scientists could study sleep patterns and overall health performance.[121][132] Scientists planned to test immunity biomarkers, with crew providing saliva samples before, during, and after the mission to test their immune system and how they were affected by radiation, isolation, and the distance away from Earth during deep space flight. This mission also intended to allow astronauts and scientists to understand space weather that will be faced in future missions, as well as how humans can survive and sustain themselves in space.[121]Optical Communications System modules on the Orion spacecraft Optical communications Artemis II tested and demonstrated optical communications to and from Earth using the Orion Artemis II Optical Communications System (O2O), which uses laser beams. Such an optical system is smaller and lighter than conventional radio and can use less power, while increasing the rate of transmission.[133] The O2O hardware is integrated into the Orion spacecraft and included an optical module (a 4-inch [100 mm] telescope and two gimbals), a modem and control electronics.[133] O2O communicated with ground stations in California and New Mexico.[133] The test device sent data to Earth with an uplink rate of up to 260 megabits per second.[134] CubeSat secondary payloads CubeSats Integration NASA's CubeSat Launch Initiative (CSLI) originally sought proposals in August 2019 from U.S. institutions and companies to fly CubeSat missions as secondary payloads aboard the SLS on the Artemis II mission.[135][136] NASA planned to accept 6-unit (12 kg, 26 lb) and 12-unit (20 kg, 44 lb) CubeSats,[137] which would be mounted on the inside of the stage adapter ring between the SLS upper stage and the Orion spacecraft, deploying after Orion separated into high Earth orbit.[137] Although selections were initially expected by February 2020,[135] all secondary payloads were removed from the mission plans in October 2021.[138] In September 2024, NASA announced that it would fly five CubeSats from international partners aboard the Artemis II mission; this was later reduced to four CubeSats. The payloads, selected from nations that are signatories to the Artemis Accords, are intended to advance global scientific and technological research while broadening international access to deep space.[139] The first CubeSat selected was Germany's TACHELES to examine the impact of space conditions on electrical components used in lunar vehicles.[140] In May 2025, NASA announced that it had selected the ATENEA satellite from Argentina's National Space Activities Commission to join the mission, with goals to study radiation shielding, map the surrounding radiation environment, gather GPS data for mission planning, and test a long-distance communication system.[141] The third and fourth satellites are K-RadCube from the Korea AeroSpace Administration to study a material simulating human tissue for the effects of space radiation, and SHAMS (or SHMS,[142] also referred to as Space Weather CubeSat-1) which means "Sun" in Arabic, from the Saudi Space Agency to measure aspects of space weather in high Earth orbit.[143][144] The fifth CubeSat bay was occupied by an avionics unit.[145] ATENEA, which was never designed to boost itself, burned up after a single orbit, having completed its mission objectives.[146][147] The other three cubesats were meant to fire propulsion systems to raise their perigee and stay in orbit. However, only SHAMS was successfully able to do so, the others burned up in the atmosphere.[148][149] Public outreach Boarding pass bearing a name that flew around the Moon on the Artemis II mission To support public outreach, NASA provided an online program allowing members of the public to generate a "boarding pass" for the mission, the names submitted were stored on an SD card subsequently flown on the spacecraft.[150] Rise, the zero-gravity indicator On March 7, 2025, NASA announced the Artemis II zero-gravity indicator (ZGI) design challenge to create a mascot for the mission.[151] The competition received more than 2,600 submissions from over 50 countries, with a total of US$23,275 awarded to the winner and 24 finalists.[152] At a pre-launch ceremony at Kennedy Space Center on March 27, 2026, astronaut Christina Koch announced Rise, designed by eight-year-old Lucas Ye of Mountain View, California, as the winning entry.[81][153] Inspired by the Apollo 8 Earthrise photograph, Rise depicts the Moon wearing Earth as a baseball cap. NASA fabricated the plush toy out of flight-safe materials and it was tethered inside the crew cabin. The SD card containing names from the boarding pass program was sewn inside Rise.[154] At the end of the mission, the crew was supposed to leave Rise in Integrity for later retrieval, but Wiseman said he felt bad leaving it behind, so he placed it inside his dry bag. Rise was displayed by the crew onboard the recovery ship and at postflight press conferences.[155] On March 4, 2026, NASA published the astronauts' menu for Artemis II. During launch and landing, the crew could consume only ready-to-eat foods, while in orbit they rehydrated freeze-dried meals using Orion's water dispenser and heated items with a briefcase-style food warmer. The four-person crew selected the menu following preflight taste tests, balancing nutritional requirements with storage constraints. Selected items include 58 tortillas, vegetable quiche, barbecued brisket, and spicy green beans; the menu also incorporated five Canadian products in recognition of the mission's Canadian crew member. Additional provisions included 43 servings of coffee, five varieties of hot sauce, and condiments such as maple syrup, peanut butter, mustard, jam, honey, and Nutella.[156][157] The crew in a group hug with "Rise" floating nearby. The crew's actions came to be known as Moon joy. The mission was broadcast by NASA throughout its duration, including live video from the spacecraft and interviews with the crew. The crew's vivid live descriptions of their observations and friendly interactions during the mission led NASA to use the term moon joy, which the agency defined in a social media post as "the feeling of intense happiness and excitement that only comes from a mission to the Moon".[158][159][160] "If you can't take love to the stars, then what are we doing? [...] That's why we send humans instead of robots sometimes, that's why we have that firsthand witness." NASA invited people before the launch to participate in citizen science observation of the Moon during the mission, looking out for impact flashes of meteors hitting the Moon. These will be compared with the observations made by the crew during their flyby.[162] In culture While Artemis II was not widely anticipated or followed prior to its launch,[163][164] worldwide interest in the mission grew quickly after launch.[165] The mission also developed collective effervescence, particularly through the crew's personal and inclusive engagement.[166][167][168] During the mission, only about one-fourth of the world population was old enough to have witnessed the last crewed mission to lunar space, Apollo 17 in 1972.[169] Misinformation On social media, many conspiracy theorists shared AI-generated videos of Artemis II and others claimed the mission was fake.[170][171][172] An enhanced photograph of a "colorful Moon" (amplifying the slight color variations in the lunar surface caused by iron and titanium deposits) was spread online and incorrectly attributed to the crew of Artemis II, but was actually taken in August 2025 by Ukrainian amateur astrophotographer Ildar Ibatullin.[173] Similar missions NASA has described Artemis II's mission objectives as comparable to those of both Apollo 7 and Apollo 8, combining tests of the spacecraft in both Earth and lunar orbit into a single mission. However, unlike Apollo 8, Artemis II did not enter lunar orbit due to performance limitations with the ESM, which cannot independently depart from low lunar orbit.[174][175][176] Instead, Artemis II flew around the Moon on a free-return trajectory, like Apollo 13 in 1970. Also, while Apollo 13 came within 158 miles (254 km) of the lunar surface, Artemis II made its closest approach at approximately 4,607 miles (7,414 km), a little over two lunar diameters. Mission insignia Comparison of the Apollo 8 and Artemis II mission patches The Artemis II mission patch, designed by Gregory Manchess,[177] has been interpreted as drawing inspiration from the Apollo 8 patch, which featured a vertically oriented infinity symbol alluding to the number 8 and representing the spacecraft's lunar orbit. Similarly, the Artemis II design incorporates a stylized trajectory resembling a half-period curve that subtly evokes the number 2 and corresponds to the first leg of the mission from Earth toward the Moon. The depiction of Earth from beyond the Moon's far side, using a perspective similar to that of the Earthrise photograph from Apollo 8, is descriptive of the mission's distance from Earth. Together, these shared design elements show the parallels between the two missions and support Artemis II's role as the first crewed lunar mission since the Apollo program.[178] During the mission, it was revealed that the astronauts had a double-sided version of the patch made. The reverse side, shown during the return to Earth, featuring an inverted design in which the Earth appeared larger and the Moon was shown in the distance.[179] Wake-up calls NASA began the tradition of playing music to astronauts during the Gemini program, and first used music to wake up a flight crew during Apollo 15. Each track is specially chosen, often by the astronauts' families, and usually has a special meaning to an individual member of the crew, or is applicable to their daily activities.[180] Artemis II became the first NASA mission to have its wake-up songs compiled into an official Spotify playlist.[181][182] In addition, pre-recorded greetings by former Apollo astronauts Charlie Duke and Jim Lovell were also broadcast to the crew,[183][184] with Lovell recording his message shortly before his death in August 2025.[184] According to Spotify, several songs chosen as wake-up calls had a sudden increase in global streams following the mission. [R5] What is Artemis II?
(https://airandspace.si.edu/stories/editorial/what-artemis-ii)
"""
Soon, four astronauts will launch on a mission to the vicinity of the Moon for the first time in over 50 years. The mission, called Artemis II, is the second mission — and first crewed mission — of NASA’s Artemis program. NASA is currently targeting Wednesday, April 1, 2026 for the launch. The April 1 launch window opens at 6:24 pm and lasts 120 minutes. NASA has also identified additional launch windows between April 2 and April 6.Here’s what to expect from the Artemis II mission. This banner hanging on a fence at Launch Complex 39B was signed by NASA employees and contractors involved in the Artemis II mission. The 10-day Artemis II mission will fly around the Moon and is considered a test flight of NASA’s Exploration Ground Systems (the systems involved in launching and recovery rockets and spacecraft), the SLS rocket, and the Orion spacecraft.As a follow-up mission to the uncrewed Artemis I mission in 2022, this crewed mission will allow NASA to flight test Orion’s life-support systems with astronauts on board. This is essential as NASA intends to send astronauts on longer-duration missions as the Artemis program goes on.Who is the Artemis II Crew?Unlike the Apollo program, which launched crews of three, the Artemis program will feature a crew of four astronauts: commander Reid Wiseman, pilot Victor Glover, mission specialist Christina Koch, and mission specialist Jeremy Hansen. This historic crew features many "firsts." Click on their names to learn more about each of their careers.Reid Wiseman, Commander: Wiseman is a Navy veteran and pilot who went to space for the first time in 2014. During that mission, he conducted two spacewalks, totaling 13 hours outside the spacecraft. He recently served as Chief of NASA’s Astronaut Office.Victor Glover, Pilot: Glover is a Navy pilot who took his first trip to space in 2020 as mission pilot of the SpaceX Crew-1 mission to the International Space Station, during which he conducted four spacewalks. Glover will be the first person of color to go to the Moon. Christina Koch, Mission Specialist: Koch worked as an electrical engineer at NASA Goddard and Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory before becoming a NASA astronaut. She launched on an 11-month mission in 2019, during which she conducted six spacewalks. Koch will be the first woman to go to the Moon.Jeremy Hansen, Mission Specialist: Hansen was a fighter pilot in the Royal Canadian Air Force before becoming a Canadian Space Agency astronaut. Artemis II will be his first spaceflight. Hansen will be the first Canadian to go to the Moon. The Artemis II crew (left to right: Jeremy Hansen, Victor Glover, Christina Koch, Reid Wiseman) pose with Apollo 11 command module Columbia during a visit to the National Air and Space Museum in 2023. Want to learn more about the crew? Check out this blog and keep an eye out for blogs about each of the astronauts in the coming weeks.How Does Artemis II Compare to Apollo? When comparing the Artemis program with NASA’s first Moon landing program, Apollo, the closest analog for Artemis II is Apollo 8. Much like Artemis II, Apollo was the first mission in the Apollo program (and in fact, ever) to send humans to the Moon. One major difference between Apollo 8 and Artemis II, however, is that while Apollo 8 orbited the Moon, Artemis II will fly around the Moon but not enter lunar orbit.Artemis II is anticipated to break Apollo 13’s record (248,655 miles) for the farthest distance humans have traveled from Earth. The Artemis II Orion spacecraft will travel over 4,000 miles beyond the Moon as it flies a loop around the Moon’s far side.What Kind of Spacecraft Carries the Crew of Artemis II? An Orion spacecraft will transport the Artemis II astronauts to the vicinity of the Moon and back. It is capsule-shaped like the Mercury, Gemini, and Apollo spacecrafts and seats four astronauts.Orion has been in development for over 20 years, originally part of an earlier lunar exploration program called Constellation. The crew of Artemis II named their Orion spacecraft Integrity to honor the trust among the people who are bringing this mission to life. The name is also a reference to the massive effort to integrate more than 300,000 individual components into a single spacecraft by teams working across the world.What Kind of Rocket Launches Artemis II?The Artemis II mission will launch using the SLS (Space Launch System) rocket. SLS, which launched for the first time in November 2022 during the Artemis I mission, can launch astronauts aboard the Orion spacecraft and cargo into space. This heavy-lift launch vehicle was developed using a combination of components from the Space Shuttle program (specifically solid rocket boosters and RS-25 engines) and new technology. The Artemis II rocket waits on the launch pad. SLS is one of the most powerful rockets to ever launch, providing 8.8 million pounds of thrust (that’s 15% more thrust than the Saturn V Moon rocket). It is powered by two solid rocket boosters (white) and a core stage (orange) with four RS-25 engines. Two minutes after launch, the solid rocket boosters will separate from the core stage and fall back to Earth, splashing into the ocean. (While the Space Shuttle’s solid rocket boosters were reusable, SLS’s are not, and NASA will not be recovering these solid rocket boosters from the ocean.) The core stage will separate from the Orion spacecraft eight minutes after launch (this happens shortly after “main engine cut off,” or MECO). The core stage will reenter the atmosphere over the Pacific Ocean.What is Artemis II’s Flight Path? The Artemis II mission will fly a figure-eight pattern that will take the crew around the Earth and then around the Moon. Watch an animation of the flight path. Flight Day 1: Artemis II will begin to fly around the Earth, first in low-Earth orbit and then into a high elliptical orbit. While flying around the Earth, the crew will conduct a 23-hour checkout of the Orion spacecraft – this allows them to test the system while they are still close by the Earth in case any unexpected issues arise. This will include an opportunity for the crew to pilot the spacecraft in manual mode.Flight Day 2: Orion’s main engine will conduct a trans-lunar injection burn, which sets Orion on its path toward the Moon. This burn also puts the spacecraft on its figure-eight free-return trajectory, which means that the Moon’s gravity will eventually guide the Orion spacecraft back to the Earth even without onboard propulsion. This provides a safeguard should Orion experience engine failure.Flight Days 3-5: Orion and its crew continue on their journey towards the Moon, entering the "lunar sphere of influence" on Day 5.Flight Day 6: Orion spacecraft Integrity performs a lunar flyby and conducts both its closest approach to the Moon and its furthest distance from Earth. During the lunar flyby, the crew will make observations and take photos of the far side of the Moon.Flight Day 7-9: Orion spacecraft leaves the “lunar sphere of influence” and begins its journey back to Earth, which includes the first two return trajectory correction burns.Flight Day 10: Orion performs its final return trajectory correction burn and the crew prepares for reentry, conducting the reentry checklist. The Orion crew module and service module separate. The Orion spacecraft reenters Earth’s atmosphere and splashes down in the Pacific Ocean with the help of three main parachutes. A U.S. Navy ship will recover Integrity and the crew, welcoming them back to Earth.Does Artemis II Have a Mission Patch? Like the great space missions that came before it, Artemis II has an official mission patch that will be worn by the crew on the sleeves of their Orion Crew Survival System spacesuits. The patch designates the mission AII, using the roman numeral for 2 to also evoke the visual of the word “All.” It’s a common refrain amongst the crew and other mission support staff at NASA that this mission is “an endeavor of discovery that seeks to explore for all and by all." The Artemis II mission patch, held here by the mission's crew, emphasizes that NASA and its partners are "All In" on the mission. The red line that helps form the A and swirls around the Moon and Earth is representative of the mission’s flight path, which includes the spacecraft flying around both our home planet and its only natural satellite. The depiction of the Earth and the Moon on the path can also be seen as an artistic variation on Apollo 8’s famous "Earthrise" photo, linking that historic mission of the 1960s to this modern-day Moon mission of the 2020s.
