Soon, four astronauts will launch on a mission to the vicinity of the Moon for the first time in over 50 years. The mission, called Artemis II, is the second mission — and first crewed mission — of NASA’s Artemis program. NASA is currently targeting Wednesday, April 1, 2026 for the launch. This banner hanging on a fence at Launch Complex 39B was signed by NASA employees and contractors involved in the Artemis II mission. The 10-day Artemis II mission will fly around the Moon and is considered a test flight of NASA’s Exploration Ground Systems (the systems involved in launching and recovery rockets and spacecraft), the SLS rocket, and the Orion spacecraft.As a follow-up mission to the uncrewed Artemis I mission in 2022, this crewed mission will allow NASA to flight test Orion’s life-support systems with astronauts on board. This historic crew features many "firsts." Click on their names to learn more about each of their careers.Reid Wiseman, Commander: Wiseman is a Navy veteran and pilot who went to space for the first time in 2014. During that mission, he conducted two spacewalks, totaling 13 hours outside the spacecraft. He recently served as Chief of NASA’s Astronaut Office.Victor Glover, Pilot: Glover is a Navy pilot who took his first trip to space in 2020 as mission pilot of the SpaceX Crew-1 mission to the International Space Station, during which he conducted four spacewalks. Christina Koch, Mission Specialist: Koch worked as an electrical engineer at NASA Goddard and Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory before becoming a NASA astronaut. An Orion spacecraft will transport the Artemis II astronauts to the vicinity of the Moon and back. It is capsule-shaped like the Mercury, Gemini, and Apollo spacecrafts and seats four astronauts.Orion has been in development for over 20 years, originally part of an earlier lunar exploration program called Constellation. SLS, which launched for the first time in November 2022 during the Artemis I mission, can launch astronauts aboard the Orion spacecraft and cargo into space. This heavy-lift launch vehicle was developed using a combination of components from the Space Shuttle program (specifically solid rocket boosters and RS-25 engines) and new technology. The Artemis II rocket waits on the launch pad. SLS is one of the most powerful rockets to ever launch, providing 8.8 million pounds of thrust (that’s 15% more thrust than the Saturn V Moon rocket). It is powered by two solid rocket boosters (white) and a core stage (orange) with four RS-25 engines. Two minutes after launch, the solid rocket boosters will separate from the core stage and fall back to Earth, splashing into the ocean. (While the Space Shuttle’s solid rocket boosters were reusable, SLS’s are not, and NASA will not be recovering these solid rocket boosters from the ocean.) The core stage will separate from the Orion spacecraft eight minutes after launch (this happens shortly after “main engine cut off,” or MECO). The core stage will reenter the atmosphere over the Pacific Ocean.What is Artemis II’s Flight Path? The Artemis II mission will fly a figure-eight pattern that will take the crew around the Earth and then around the Moon. Watch an animation of the flight path. Flight Day 1: Artemis II will begin to fly around the Earth, first in low-Earth orbit and then into a high elliptical orbit. While flying around the Earth, the crew will conduct a 23-hour checkout of the Orion spacecraft – this allows them to test the system while they are still close by the Earth in case any unexpected issues arise. This will include an opportunity for the crew to pilot the spacecraft in manual mode.Flight Day 2: Orion’s main engine will conduct a trans-lunar injection burn, which sets Orion on its path toward the Moon. This burn also puts the spacecraft on its figure-eight free-return trajectory, which means that the Moon’s gravity will eventually guide the Orion spacecraft back to the Earth even without onboard propulsion. This provides a safeguard should Orion experience engine failure.Flight Days 3-5: Orion and its crew continue on their journey towards the Moon, entering the "lunar sphere of influence" on Day 5.Flight Day 6: Orion spacecraft Integrity performs a lunar flyby and conducts both its closest approach to the Moon and its furthest distance from Earth. During the lunar flyby, the crew will make observations and take photos of the far side of the Moon.Flight Day 7-9: Orion spacecraft leaves the “lunar sphere of influence” and begins its journey back to Earth, which includes the first two return trajectory correction burns.Flight Day 10: Orion performs its final return trajectory correction burn and the crew prepares for reentry, conducting the reentry checklist. The Orion crew module and service module separate. The Orion spacecraft reenters Earth’s atmosphere and splashes down in the Pacific Ocean with the help of three main parachutes. A U.S. Navy ship will recover Integrity and the crew, welcoming them back to Earth.Does Artemis II Have a Mission Patch? Like the great space missions that came before it, Artemis II has an official mission patch that will be worn by the crew on the sleeves of their Orion Crew Survival System spacesuits. The patch designates the mission AII, using the roman numeral for 2 to also evoke the visual of the word “All.” It’s a common refrain amongst the crew and other mission support staff at NASA that this mission is “an endeavor of discovery that seeks to explore for all and by all." The Artemis II mission patch, held here by the mission's crew, emphasizes that NASA and its partners are "All In" on the mission. The red line that helps form the A and swirls around the Moon and Earth is representative of the mission’s flight path, which includes the spacecraft flying around both our home planet and its only natural satellite. The depiction of the Earth and the Moon on the path can also be seen as an artistic variation on Apollo 8’s famous "Earthrise" photo, linking that historic mission of the 1960s to this modern-day Moon mission of the 2020s.
