What’s going on here? Saudi chemicals group SABIC booked a 2025 net loss of 25.8 billion riyals ($6.9 billion) after writedowns tied to selling overseas units, even as it improved cash generation and set a $3.5–$4.0 billion 2026 investment budget. What does this mean? SABIC swung from a 2024 profit to a deep loss mainly because it took impairment and disposal charges as it exited parts of Europe and the Americas. That matters because petrochemicals are stuck in a global overcapacity funk, squeezing prices, margins, and plant utilization – and revenue slipped 1% to 116.5 billion riyals as average selling prices fell. On an underlying basis, the business still softened but didn’t cr..ater: adjusted net income fell to about $550 million from $1.57 billion. The sales are already in motion, too, including a deal to sell its European petrochemicals business to AEQUITA and its engineering thermoplastics operations in the Americas and Europe to Mutares. With Saudi Aramco owning 70% of SABIC, the logic looks like concentrating on cost-advantaged assets that can hold up better while the market stays oversupplied. Why should I care? For markets: Cash flow tells you who can endure the slump. In commodity downcycles, investors often look past headline losses and focus on who can fund operations and capex. SABIC’s free cash flow rose 17% to 7.2 billion riyals, helped by tighter working capital and lower spending. But it also declared 9 billion riyals in 2025 dividends, so the real test is how comfortably those payouts sit alongside the 2026 capex guide. The bigger picture: Overcapacity is redrawing where chemicals get made. Stepping back from higher-cost footprints is becoming a theme as oversupply lingers. SABIC’s exits suggest scale matters less than being integrated and low-cost – and if peers follow, the industry could see more consolidation and more investment flowing to regions with cheaper feedstocks and stronger value-chain links.
