Runners who ran within 5 minutes of a Boston Qualifying (BQ) time waited to see what the 2026 Boston Marathon cutoff time would be. Shana Miller, a 54-year-old Boston-based running coach, felt like she had given herself just enough room, but when she saw that this year’s cutoff was 4:34, her stomach dropped. She was off by 4 seconds. “In a nutshell, I was heartbroken,” she told Outside Run. She joined 9,000 other runners who didn’t make it. Get the RUN Newsletter The cutoff time this year is something of a Rorschach test. Some runners expected the cutoff time to be much lower due to the new qualifying times in place this year. Other runners, myself included, expected a cutoff time above 5:00. One thing is for certain, though. This is the hardest it has been to get into the Boston Marathon in 40 years. From 1980 to 1986, the qualifying time for open division runners was 2:50 for men and 3:20 for women. This year, an open division runner needed to run 2:50:26/3:20:26 to make the cutoff. Let’s take a look at the data to understand better how we arrived at this point, how the cutoff time is affecting the field, and what the future holds. How the Cutoff Time Is Calculated Calculating the cutoff time is a fairly simple mathematical question. First, a given number of runners apply during the registration period. This year, that number was 33,249. Next, the Boston Athletic Association (BAA) determines how many runners to accept. This year, BAA accepted 24,362 applicants. If this is less than the number of applicants, a cutoff time will be required. Finally, runners are lined up according to how many seconds they are under their qualifying time, commonly called their buffer. Then, simply find the point at which only the accepted number of applicants is beneath the cutoff time. This year, that was 4:34. While this math is simple, there is a complex web of factors that determine exactly where this cutoff time falls. How many runners qualify, which runners apply, and how many spots are available all matter. If any or all of them shift one way or the other, it will influence the outcome. In 2024, the BAA accepted 24,069 runners to the 2025 Boston Marathon who ran at least 6:51 faster than the qualifying time of their respective gender and age group. Photo: Getty ImagesThe Impact of the New Qualifying Times John Morse of Annapolis, Maryland, qualified for Boston for the 11th time, running a 2:46. He feels for runners who ran a BQ time only to get rejected by the cutoff. “It’s not right that the qualifying time is so far off,” Morse said. “Every year it seems we have the same discussion about how the BAA needs to set realistic expectations for the qualifying time that accounts for the anticipated field size.” He’s right that the biggest factor that BAA can control is the qualifying standards. Adjusting these up or down will change the percentage of runners who meet their qualifying times. Since the 1980s, BAA has relaxed these standards several times. But demand for the race increased, and 2012 saw the first cutoff time in Boston history. To contain it, they implemented stricter qualifying times in 2013 and 2020. After a record-setting year in 2025, they once again tightened things up. All runners under 60 saw their qualifying times reduced by 5 minutes. This change gave runners hope that the cutoff time would be significantly less than it was last year (6:51). These new qualifying times should have reduced the number of qualifying runners by about 20 percent. But the change in applicants from 2025 to 2026 was nowhere near that large. That trend continued this year. Marathon participation, too, has seen spikes in popularity. From 2000 to 2010, the combined number of finishers across the 25 largest marathons in the United States increased from 180,000 to 280,000. Throughout this time period, the field size at the Boston Marathon was also slowly growing, accommodating new Boston hopefuls year after year. Photo: Brian Rock with FlourishFrom 2012 to 2014, marathon participation in the country started to plateau. Shortly thereafter, the Boston Marathon reached a field size of approximately 30,000 entrants and stopped growing. From 2015 to 2019, the number of finishers across all marathons was slowly declining. Yet the number of applicants to Boston was still increasing—as evidenced by the increasing number of rejected applicants and the growing cutoff time. With the size of the race stagnant, Boston couldn’t accommodate any more runners. The increased demand at this point came from international runners. The share of international runners at the race was only 15 percent in 2012. It increased to 25 percent in 2019, and it has consistently been above 30 percent the last few years. In the years since COVID, the number of marathon finishers in the United States is once again on the rise. It took a couple of years to fully rebound, but in 2024, there were almost as many marathon finishers as there were at the 2014 peak. Over the course of the qualifying period for the 2026 Boston Marathon, the number of finishers has increased by another 10 percent—pushing participation levels to new heights. The result of this growth is that the number of qualifying runners didn’t drop by 20 percent. It only dropped about 6-7 percent. This created a worst-case scenario where the cutoff time could be as high as 5:30 or 6:00. Who Actually Applied to Run? There’s another factor that has a big influence on the final cutoff time–which of those qualifying runners actually decide to apply. Based on the number of qualifying runners, if a similar percentage of eligible runners applied to run the 2026 Boston Marathon, there should have been between 34,000 and 34,500 applicants. There were only 33,249 applicants. This was about 8.6 percent lower than the number of applicants last year. This decline outpaced the decline in the number of qualifiers, and it suggested that the cutoff time would be lower, in the range of 5:00 to 5:30. There’s been a lot of speculation that the current political climate in the United States may have depressed international participation in this year’s race. It turns out this is not the case. When asked if there was any decline or difference in international applicants this year, Jack Fleming, President and CEO of the BAA, told Outside Run in a statement, “The 129th and 130th Boston Marathon application process both received 37 percent international applicants vs. 63 percent domestic. Far fewer of them applied than the data suggested they would. The reasons aren’t entirely clear, but this dip shifted the overall distribution of applicants. If the applicant pool had been more evenly spread out, the slowest qualifiers would have made up a smaller share, likely resulting in a cutoff closer to 5:20–5:30. Instead, because a higher proportion of applicants fell into the 0–5 minute buffer range, the cutoff had to drop lower to trim the same number of runners. How Many Runners Did the BAA Accept? The final factor is the exact number of runners that will be accepted in a given year. There are logistical constraints as to how many runners BAA can accommodate, but this decision to increase or decrease the field size can have a significant impact on the cutoff time. “Acceptances are based on our field size of 30,000 and how many we can accommodate in our open field alongside our charity runners, the professional, para, and adaptive field and partner entries,” Fleming explained. In 2024, when the number of applicants skyrocketed, only 22,000 runners were accepted, and there was a 5:31 cutoff. Last year saw another record number of applicants. Had the field of accepted runners remained at around 22,000, the cutoff time would have been closer to 8:00. BAA’s decision to increase the field to 24,000 reduced that to 6:51. The number of accepted applicants last year was already one of the highest in the history of the Boston Marathon. The net effect of the tougher qualifying times and an equally applied deep cutoff is that there will be fewer younger runners in the Boston Marathon and more older runners. For years, the race has been trending toward more masters runners, but that trend will likely be even stronger this year. What Does the Future Hold? It’s too early to predict with any degree of certainty what registration for the 2027 Boston Marathon will be like, but it is possible to sketch out a few different futures. The new downhill results index will reduce the number of runners qualifying at downhill races, and the elimination of the double-dipping period at the beginning of September (i.e., the rule that said runners who qualified for the Boston Marathon could use the same race result to apply for two consecutive Boston Marathons), will also reduce the number of qualifiers. Neither of the changes are likely to have a huge impact, but they’ll likely push the baseline cutoff down to the high 3:00s or low 4:00s. Double-digit growth—like we saw last year—could lead to a cutoff time of 6:00 or more. This is a stretch. But it’s possible.
