The golden ratio is defined as the proportion in which a line segment is divided into two unequal parts such that the ratio of the whole segment to the longer part is equal to the ratio of the longer part to the shorter. In algebraic terms, if the shorter part of the segment is taken as one unit and the longer part as x units, this forms the equation ( x + 1 ) x = x 1 . History Golden spiral with rectanglesA golden spiral traced through a series of golden rectangles, each smaller and differently colored, illustrates how the golden ratio repeats at ever-decreasing scales. The spiral follows the curve created by successively removing squares from each rectangle.The origins of the golden ratio can be traced to the ancient Greeks. Mathematician Euclid, in his work Elements (c. 300 bce), described the division of a line “in extreme and mean ratio,” a division in which the ratio of the whole length to the longer part equals the ratio of the longer part to the shorter—the proportion now known as the golden ratio. During the Renaissance interest in the ratio was revived. Italian mathematician Luca Pacioli published De divina proportione (1509; “Divine Proportion”), a treatise that celebrated the ratio’s supposed harmony. The book was illustrated by polymath Leonardo da Vinci, whose works have often been analyzed for evidence of golden ratio proportions. German astronomer–mathematician Johannes Kepler, in Harmonices Mundi (1619; “Harmony of the Worlds”), referred to the ratio as proportio divina (“divine proportion”) and noted that the ratios of successive Fibonacci numbers—a sequence in which each number is the sum of the two before it—converge to the golden ratio.
