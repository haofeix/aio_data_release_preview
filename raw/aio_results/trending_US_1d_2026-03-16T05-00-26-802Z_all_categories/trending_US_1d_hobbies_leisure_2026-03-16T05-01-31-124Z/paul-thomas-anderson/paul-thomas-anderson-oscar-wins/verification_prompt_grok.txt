Since his arrival on the scene during the '90s indie boom, Paul Thomas Anderson has been a prominent cinematic voice, one of the few filmmakers still able to command sizable budgets for idiosyncratic projects that push boundaries and break new ground, films like Boogie Nights, Magnolia, There Will Be Blood, and Phantom Thread. But until now — in the eyes of the Academy, at least — Anderson was only a 14-time nominee. That all changed at the 98th Academy Awards, where Anderson headed into the evening as the writer and director of the odds-on favorite for Best Picture and recipient of 13 Oscar nominations, One Battle After Another. And now, a little more than 30 years after his first film debuted at the Sundance Film Festival, Paul Thomas Anderson is an Oscar winner. Make that "three-time Oscar winner." The writer-director won what would only be his first of the night in Best Adapted Screenplay, before winning in the two other categories for which he was nominated — Director and Picture. It's that Best Director win, however, that has solidified a level of achievement among directors that no one else has ever matched. Before the Academy Awards, Anderson was the only filmmaker to have directing prizes from the "Big Three" international film festivals: Cannes, Berlin, and Venice. (Akira Kurosawa, Martin Scorsese, and Emir Kusturica have a measly two apiece.) Now, Anderson is the only director who can lay claim to all three and an Oscar. For much of Anderson's early career, it was only the Academy's writers branch that recognized him, nominating him in Best Original Screenplay in 1998 and 2000 for Boogie Nights and Magnolia, respectively. It was with his 2007 film, There Will Be Blood, that Anderson began to see wider recognition from the Academy. The loose Upton Sinclair adaptation scored him the same trio of nominations that One Battle earned nearly 20 years later — Adapted Screenplay, Directing, and Picture — all three of which he lost to Joel and Ethan Coen for There Will Be Blood's major rival of the evening, No Country for Old Men. Writers continued to honor Anderson, nominating him again for 2015's Inherent Vice, the first of what would become his two Thomas Pynchon adaptations after One Battle. Phantom Thread brought him back into the Best Director and Picture conversation, curiously missing an Original Screenplay nomination. Licorice Pizza scored him three more nominations, once again honoring him as a writer, director, and producer with Best Picture.
