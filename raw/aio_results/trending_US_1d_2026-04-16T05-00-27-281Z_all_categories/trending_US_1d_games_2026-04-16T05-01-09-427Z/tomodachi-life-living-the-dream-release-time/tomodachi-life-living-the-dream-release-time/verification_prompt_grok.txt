From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia Tomodachi Life: Living the DreamDeveloperNintendo EPD[a]PublisherNintendoDirectorRyutaro TakahashiProducerYoshio SakamotoDesignersHayuru SomaKanako TsugihashiEisaku NakaeProgrammersNaonori OhnishiTakaomi UenoArtistDaisuke KageyamaWriterNoriyuki SatoComposersToru MinegishiShinobu NagataReika NakaiKairi HamadaPlatformNintendo SwitchReleaseApril 16, 2026GenreSocial simulationModeSingle-player Tomodachi Life: Living the Dream, known in Japan as Tomodachi Collection: Exciting Life,[b] is a 2026 social simulation game developed by Nintendo EPD for the Nintendo Switch. It is the third entry in the Tomodachi Life series, succeeding Tomodachi Collection (2009) and Tomodachi Life (2013). It revolves around user-customizable Mii characters who live on an island overseen by the player, who can give them items and influence their relationships. It introduces more customization options for character creation, pronouns, and dating preferences. Living the Dream received positive critical reception for its gameplay, art style, and humor, but criticism for its lack of image and Mii sharing. Screenshot of a Mii (based on Wikipe-tan), who is inside their home Tomodachi Life: Living the Dream is a social simulation game[1] that revolves around the everyday lives of Miis, user-customizable avatars, who reside on a remote tropical island.[2][3] Gameplay starts with the player tasked with naming the island[4] before either creating Miis from scratch or a set of prompts, importing them from the Switch's creation software, or using an existing Mii as a reference.[5] The player can adjust their romantic preferences and give them a personality by selecting various temperament attributes.[6][7] Miis speak through a robotic speech synthesis that can be modified.[8] A maximum of 70 Miis can live on the island at once.[9][10] Upon creation, the Miis settle in their individual houses.[11] Occasionally, Miis will signal the player that they have a problem.[12] This ranges from wanting food or clothes, advice on relationships, decorations and player participation in minigames.[11] Fulfilling their needs boosts their level, awarding the player with in-game currency and warm fuzzies that can be used at a fountain to grant wishes and increase the island's rank.[12] Upon a Mii leveling up, the player can gift them items, quirks, and catchphrases.[13] Living the Dream introduces the ability to customize the layout and shape of the island. The game allows players to directly control interactions between Miis rather than relying on random chance for Miis to develop their relationships.[14] Additionally, options for the in-game Mii Maker allow more advanced customization, including options for hair, facial features, and ears.[15][16] Living the Dream allows non-binary Miis, same-sex relationships, and aromantic Miis.[17] It is possible to customize the Miis' personal pronouns.[citation needed] Time in the game passes parallel to that of the real-world clock.[1] Living the Dream started development in 2017. While finishing development on Miitomo, Ryutaro Takahashi and Yoshio Sakamoto were inspired to develop a new entry in the series, citing limitations in Tomodachi Life (2013). Wanting the Mii characters to visually look more appealing, the team revised their appearances with a "simple, anime-inspired toon-style."[18] The expanded scope came from the increased processing power of the Nintendo Switch, as it allowed Miis to explore a more expansive area instead of being confined to a singular apartment building on a small island.[18] Additionally, the developers aimed to make a bigger game that was not just an expanded version of Tomodachi Life so players would not get tired and feel like they had seen everything too early on.[18] Tomodachi Life: Living the Dream was announced at the end of a Nintendo Direct presentation on March 27, 2025.[19] It was one of the new Nintendo Switch games revealed alongside Rhythm Heaven Groove, with both being the first new entries in their respective series in over a decade.[20][21] The announcement trailer posted to X by Nintendo in Japan became one of their most liked posts, surpassing that of the Nintendo Switch 2 reveal.[22] A second Nintendo Direct presentation on September 12, 2025 introduced the creation of Miis and their interactions. It revealed the initial release window for Spring of 2026.[23] A 22-minute Direct presentation focused on the game was premiered on January 29, 2026.[24] The presentation included demonstrations for Mii creation tools, locations, island customization, and romantic relationships. Different videos were also broadcast in North America, Europe, and Japan featuring a different cast of Mii characters.[25][26] Hugh Morris, a jester-themed Mii exclusive to the American trailer named after the word humorous, was met with acclaim by critics.[27][28][29] In other regions, different Mii characters gained popularity, such as Bubbles and Carlo, who appeared in the European and Japanese Nintendo Directs.[30][31][32] A week after this Direct aired, the soundtrack for Tomodachi Life on the 3DS was added to the Nintendo Music mobile app.[33] The game was released for the Nintendo Switch on April 16, 2026.[19][34] The Nintendo Switch 2 is backwards compatible with the game.[35][36] It was the last video game offered for the Switch Game Vouchers program the day prior to its discontinuation, with the vouchers being unavailable to purchase after January 30, 2026.[37][38] Pre-orders in the United Kingdom included a themed magnetic photo frame.[39] In Japan, pre-orders included bonus accessories depending on the retailer purchased, including stickers of Miis, keychains, and a microfiber cloth.[40] During the month of March 2026, it was at the top of the Amazon pre-order charts in Japan.[41][42] A demo was released on March 25, giving access to a limited feature set of the retail version. It restricted Mii creation to a total of three, and introduced players to the Fresh Kingdom food shop and the Where & Wear clothing shop, among other limited game mechanics and features. Progress established in the demo can be carried over to the retail version.[43] Completing the demo unlocks one of six color variants of a hamster costume.[44] Upon its initial reveal, fans speculated whether same-gender relationships would be included.[45] Neither of its predecessors contained the ability to form relationships with Miis of the same gender, which sparked controversy ahead of the international release of the latter. Despite vocal resentment toward this decision, Nintendo did not add the feature post-launch.[46] However, they stated that if they were to create a third game in the series, they would "strive to design a gameplay experience from the ground up that is more inclusive, and better represents all players."[47][48] When Tomodachi Life: Living the Dream was announced, speculation arose on whether Nintendo would commit to their statement. Additionally, the reveal trailer featured a scene with two female Miis interacting during a dream sequence, with fans theorizing the scene hinted at additional romance options.[45][49][50] With the premiere of the game's official Direct, it was confirmed that the option for gay, bisexual, non-binary, and aromantic Miis would be present in the game.[7][17][51][52] On the same day as the game's release, ten music tracks were added to the Nintendo Music app as a "special release".[53] Fans and gaming journalists praised the inclusion of same sex relationships and non-binary Miis in Tomodachi Life: Living the Dream. Publications such as Checkpoint Gaming and The Daily Dot commented that the additions were well-received by a majority of fans.[54][55] Amelia Zollner writing for Kotaku described the settings as "surprisingly inclusive", being particularly impressed by the explicit use of the term non-binary, calling it "a massive step forward for the developer". They also noted the majority of positive comments from fans during the live stream on YouTube, with Zollner feeling relieved that it was confirmed prior to its release.[56] Olivia Richman writing for The Escapist called the addition "A big win for the LGBTQIA+ community".[57] Alana Hagues writing for Nintendo Life called the addition "a huge win", crediting the developers for sticking to their promise from 2014.[58] Jade King writing for TheGamer expressed her excitement being able to accurately represent her friends and family without needing to do any extra steps like in Tomodachi Life. King was also delighted that Nintendo was inclusive of the LGBTQ+ community, as she feels much of the video game industry is regressing from representing those topics. In her summary, she wrote that "Tomodachi Life is woke now, and [she] couldn't be more delighted".[59] However, the inclusion of queer elements was not universally welcomed, as Richman called attention to some fans who were upset over their inclusion. Other fans showed resentment toward the localization of the game and the inclusion of women with colored hair, with video game designer and right-wing activist Mark Kern erroneously suggesting in a post on Twitter/X that the recently appointed Nintendo of America CEO Devon Pritchard was behind the decision.[60][61] Upon learning that image sharing would be restricted so that "out-of-context scenes" would not be misinterpreted, the decision was met with criticism,[62][63][64] and fan response was mostly negative.[65] Dustin Bailey writing for GamesRadar+ called the decision "downright absurd", as they believed sharing character interactions with others was one of the main appeals of the previous installment.[66] Deven McClure writing for Polygon believed that restrictions on image sharing contrasted with the game's emphasis on creativity.[67] Alana Hagues writing for Nintendo Life thought the decision was strange when compared to the additional inclusivity options. She speculated that the company was attempting to avoid another Swapnote incident, which was disabled due to offensive imagery being distributed,[68] whereas Stephanie Liu of Siliconera thought the decision was made to reduce posts on social media platforms involving controversial people being recreated as Miis.[69] Additionally, the lack of online functionality was criticized. McClure thought the inability to share Miis online would potentially impact the live streaming community, as well as the game's success overall.[67] Anna Koselke of GamesRadar+ thought it was disappointing compared to other games such as StreetPass Mii Plaza (2011) and Miitopia (2016).[70] The game was also criticized for lacking the concert hall, previously included in Tomodachi Life, that allowed the player to create custom Vocaloid-style songs.[71][72] Tomodachi Life: Living the Dream received "generally favorable" reviews according to review aggregator website Metacritic.[73] Review aggregator OpenCritic assessed that 79% of critics recommended the game.[74] Critics praised the game's humor, customization, and relationship simulation mechanics.[13][77] GamesRadar+'s Emma-Jane Betts and TechRadar's Dashiell Wood both praised the game's customization, and drew connections to reality TV shows such as Love Island and Big Brother.[76][77] Amanda Kay Oaks of ComicBook.com lauded the game's humor, but felt that the gameplay got tiring quickly.[81] The decision to restrict image sharing and online Mii sharing was criticized by many reviewers.[13] Brian Shea of Game Informer called the decision "massively shortsighted," and noted that removing the online Mii sharing features also removed the "safety net" for players who didn't want to manually create many Miis.[75] Eurogamer's Alexander Bohn-Elias was also critical of the sharing restrictions, as well as the inability to use the Joy-Con 2's mouse features in the in-game editor.[79] ^ Additional development by Nintendo Studios Singapore, Intelligent Systems, and Bandai Namco Studios Malaysia ^ Japanese: トモダチコレクション わくわく生活, Hepburn: Tomodachi Korekushon Wakuwaku Seikatsu ^ Based on 70 reviews ^ Based on 34 reviews ^ Jump up to: a b Lambertsen, Carrie (15 April 2026).
