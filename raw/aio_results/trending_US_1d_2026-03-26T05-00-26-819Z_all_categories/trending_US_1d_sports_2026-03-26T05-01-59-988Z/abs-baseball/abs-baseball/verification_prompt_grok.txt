The evolving history of baseball’s strike zoneThe earliest strike zonesIn the initial days of 19th-century baseball, the strike zone was more of a suggestion, used by umpires to move the game along if batters refused to swing. It wasn't until 1887 that the first official zone was codified, set as the top of the shoulder to the bottom of the knee.The modern zone arrived in 1950, when rule changes shrank it to cover the area from the armpits to the top of the knee. It would stay put for the next 13 seasons.1963: The zone gets tallerIn 1963, partially in response to Roger Maris breaking Babe Ruth’s home run record two seasons prior, the top of the zone was expanded from the armpits to the top of the shoulder, while the bottom edge was lowered from the top of the knee to its midpoint. A larger zone, the thinking went, would allow for more strikes, faster at-bats, and fewer home runs.1969: The zone shrinks back downThe effects of the 1963-68 “big zone” worked well – too well, actually. The mid-’60s are forever remembered as a great time to be a pitcher, with one study saying that the zone change decreased scoring by 11% as strikeouts rose. While most fans remember that the reaction to 1968’s “Year of the Pitcher” was to lower the mound by five inches, the choice to revert the zone back to what it had been from 1950-62 was a factor in the rebound of offense as well.1980s and '90s: Minor adjustmentsIn 1988, the top of the zone moved from the armpits to a line “at the midpoint between the top of the shoulders and top of the pants,” or mid-torso. Eight years later, in 1996, the bottom of the zone moved from the top of the knees to just beneath the kneecap. While the instructions and evaluations given to umpires have fluctuated in the years since, the official rulebook strike zone remained untouched over the final three decades prior to ABS.Umpires have become more accurate than everThe called strike zone in 2008Contrary to popular belief, umpires have become far more consistent and accurate in the years since pitch-by-pitch tracking came online in 2008. The called strike zone (shown here against right-handed batters) was for years shorter and wider than the rulebook definition would have you believe – to say nothing of how it differed per-pitcher or per-umpire.The called strike zone in 2025By 2025, the called zone looked considerably more like the rulebook zone, and furthermore, call accuracy had gone up, too. In 2008, umpires were accurate 84.1% of the time on ball/strike calls; in 2025, that figure had increased to 92.8%. That stands out even more when you realize that as ever-increasing velocity and spin have made life harder on batters, it’s also made it more difficult to call balls and strikes, too.Players are only accurate on ABS challenges approximately half the time, and those challenges can only happen when the player feels confident enough to ask for one. Therefore, the combination of ever-more-accurate umpires and the ABS challenge system should make for the most accurately called zone baseball has ever seen.The technology behind baseball dataHow the ABS system worksSince 2020, Statcast (and now ABS) has been powered by Hawk-Eye cameras, which track the movement of the baseball as well as all the players on the field – including coaches and umpires too.Each ballpark has 12 Hawk-Eye cameras, seven of which (red) are dedicated to player tracking.Dedicated eyes on the zoneThe remaining five Hawk-Eye cameras are specifically committed to tracking the movement of the baseball. In 2023, these cameras were upgraded from 100 frames per second (fps) to 300, allowing improved ability to capture the spin, movement, velocity, and the position of the ball. This is how the ABS system can place the location of the ball within the strike zone with pinpoint precision.How the ABS strike zone is both individualized and consistentA crouching, shifting zonePreviously, umpires were forced to consider not only a batter’s height but also their stance when creating the zone they’d call. That meant that two batters of equal height could, in practice, have very different strike zones, based on how tall they stood at the plate. Therefore, batters could – and did – have slightly different strike zones each time they came to bat.A reliably measured zoneInstead of a constantly-evolving player-specific zone, estimated by human eyes, each player has their zone measured in Spring Training, standing straight up, with no shoes, hats, or crouches. For all hitters, the top end of their zone will be 53.5% of their measured height, and the bottom will be 27% of that height. Another way to think about it: Split your own height into quarters, all stacked atop one another. Your strike zone roughly consists of the second one from the bottom.Two extremes, two different zonesLet’s take two of the best hitters in the American League, who happen to be two extremely different sizes. Houston’s Jose Altuve is 5-foot-6, while Aaron Judge of the New York Yankees is 6-foot-7 – a 13-inch difference.Two players with such radically different sizes should never have the same zone, but they should have their own zone, consistently called – and now, they will.A batter-appropriate zoneWhile Altuve and Judge will have the same zone as a proportion of their heights, in practice their zones will be differently-sized and in different places – as befits a pair of players with a 13-inch height difference.Altuve’s zone will be 17.5 inches tall, and 297 square inches total.
