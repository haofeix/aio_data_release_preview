Top Questions How did the concept of zero develop in India? How did zero spread from India to the rest of the world? What role does zero play in science and technology? zero, number denoting the absence of quantity. Represented by the symbol “0,” it plays a foundational role in arithmetic, algebra, computing, and scientific measurement. It lies at the center of the number line, separating positive numbers from negative numbers, and it operates as a placeholder in positional number systems. Though now ubiquitous, the concept of zero as both a symbol and idea is a relatively late development in human history. Although placeholder symbols for absence were used in earlier systems, the modern zero—as a numeral with its own value and arithmetic rules—originated in ancient India before spreading to the Islamic world and Europe. Historical development Early placeholders What Is a Placeholder?A placeholder is a symbol used in a positional number system to show that a digit is missing from a specific place. For example, in the number 102, the 0 acts as a placeholder to indicate that there are no tens in that position, while preserving the positions of the hundreds and ones units. The earliest known use of a placeholder in a positional number system appeared in Mesopotamia, where the Sumerians, about 5,000 years ago, used a slanted double wedge in cuneiform (system of writing in the ancient Middle East) to indicate the absence of a digit. A tablet from Kish (in present-day Iraq) from about 700 bce provides evidence of the use of three hooks to indicate missing digits within a number. Between the 6th and 3rd centuries bce, the Babylonians employed symbols—typically one or two wedges—to mark empty positions between nonzero digits in their number system. In ancient Greek mathematics the concept of nothingness was acknowledged but not formalized as a number. Greek philosopher Aristotle rejected the notion of a void and argued that division by zero lacked meaning, reinforcing the absence of zero in the Greek numerical system, which relied on the Greek alphabet and was limited to positive integers. The ancient Romans also developed a nonpositional numeral system, using letters such as I, V, and X for recording values rather than calculation.
