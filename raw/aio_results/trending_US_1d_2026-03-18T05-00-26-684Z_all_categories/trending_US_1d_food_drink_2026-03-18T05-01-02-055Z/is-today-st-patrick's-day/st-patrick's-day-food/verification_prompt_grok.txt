2 min readCorned beef and cabbage is about as synonymous with St. Patrick’s Day as black-eyed peas are with New Year’s Day. This iconic dish is made with cured beef brisket and braised cabbage and is often served with crusty bread and potatoes on March 17. But how traditional is it really? As it turns out, corned beef is much more of an Irish-American culinary tradition than it is one hailing from the Emerald Isle.What Is Corned Beef?Before you can fully appreciate the history (or lack thereof) of eating corned beef and cabbage on St. Patrick’s Day, it’s important to understand exactly what this dish is. Corned beef starts with beef brisket, which is pickled for several days in a brine made with sugar and aromatic whole spices like peppercorns, caraway seeds, and mustard seeds. Once the beef has had ample brining time, it simmers in that same brine on the stovetop for several hours, which allows the flavors to deeply penetrate the meat and help to soften the tough cut of beef. It's then sliced up and typically served with braised cabbage, carrots, and potatoes for a hearty Irish-inspired feast.Do Irish People Eat Corned Beef and Cabbage?You may be surprised to learn that corned beef and cabbage isn’t actually a traditional Irish dish. Today, you’ll find beef stews and steaks on menus across Ireland, but that wasn’t always the case. Beef used to be considered a luxury ingredient in Ireland and was typically only consumed by the wealthy. Instead, many Irish families relied on preserved pork, which was much more affordable and deemed just as nutritious.That changed when Irish immigrants came to the United States and found that cured beef was readily available, affordable, and delicious. According to Irish Central, this was especially true in New York’s Lower East Side where Irish immigrants settled alongside Jewish neighbors. “The proximity of the Irish and Jewish communities, in the Lower Eastside of Manhattan in the mid-1800s and early 1900s is said to be largely responsible for the popularity of corned beef among the Irish immigrants.” Irish Americans began incorporating corned beef into their meals, and it eventually became a staple on St. Patrick’s Day in the United States. Other dishes commonly served for the holiday include lamb stew and shepherd’s pie.In Ireland, however, not much has changed. You’re much more likely to find people enjoying bacon and cabbage on the holiday as a nod to the traditional, budget-friendly meal they’ve eaten for generations.

[R7] What Are Irish Potatoes? The Iconic St. Patrick's Day Treat.
(https://www.buzzfeed.com/lukefield/how-irish-potatoes-are-made-explainer)
"""
Christmas has candy canes. Easter has Cadbury Creme Eggs. Valentine's Day has the typical box of chocolates that requires a map to navigate. But what about St. Patrick's Day? Maybe you treat yourself to a handful of Lucky Charms marshmallows, but that hardly stacks up to other holiday candy titans. However, if you grew up in the small region of Eastern Pennsylvania and Central/South New Jersey, you might be thinking, "Wait a minute! What about Irish Potatoes?!" To which I would respond, "Yeah, I know! I was just trying to create some journalistic tension! Is that okay?!" Irish Potatoes are coconut-vanilla buttercream candies, covered in cinnamon. The confection looks like a potato that's just been plucked from the ground, lumps and all. Growing up in the Philadelphia suburbs, these were an essential part of my childhood. I have distinct memories of at least one of my Catholic school teachers bringing Irish potatoes to class to celebrate great and glorious St. Patrick every year. You can find them in any grocery store in the area starting in March. Cinnamon and coconut is an odd mix and, on its face, does not scream "I'm from Ireland!" Wondering where the candy came from and why it's so regionally popular, I asked my Irish friend John (from Ireland) if he'd ever heard of them. Perhaps, then, it is just an Irish American tradition; something that developed in neighborhoods that gained more popularity over time. I asked my 76-year-old father, who is 100% Irish and was born and raised in South Philadelphia, if he had any memories of Irish Potatoes when he was a kid. He seemed to recall something like it, but Oh Ryan's didn't hit the shelves big time until 1989, so whatever he was remembering was suspect. He's 76, and he's doing his best; let's cut him a break. There was only one way I was going to learn more about Irish potatoes and how they came to be so wildly popular. I had to go to the source: the Oh Ryan's candy factory, located just south of Philadelphia in Linwood, Pennsylvania. When I arrived at the factory, I was greeted by Dave, the patriarch of this family candy-making business for the past 40 years. And it is a true family business. His wife, Laurel, works in the factory office; the two of them opened Christopher Chocolates in Newtown Square, PA, in 1986. The store's namesake is their son Christopher, who now runs the whole retail operation. Oh Ryan's Irish Potatoes are named after Dave and Laurel's other son, Ryan, who oversees the factory. The first thing I asked Dave was where the heck Irish Potatoes came from. A transplant from Buffalo, New York, Dave responded, "I had never heard of them. When we opened the store, people would come in and ask, 'Where are the Irish Potatoes?'" After some research, Dave and Laurel began hand-making them in the store. Turns out, the Irish Potato has been a Philadelphia/New Jersey staple for over 100 years, and there are many candy stores in the area that make their own versions. But Oh Ryan's Irish Potatoes are at the top of the food chain. In 1989, they made 8,000 pounds of the candy; this year, they hit approximately 50,000 pounds. Each box is only seven ounces, so you math whizzes out there can do your thing. "Let me show you the whole process," Dave said. Walking into this room, I was immediately hit with the strong scent of cinnamon. That's because it was literally everywhere. "Perfect timing," Dave said. "We just got an extra order for 40 more cases, so you'll be able to see some of the process." Oh Ryan's begins production of Irish Potatoes in mid-December to accommodate the massive demand that starts on March 1. First, a mixture of butter, fondant sugar, coconut, and other ingredients gets poured into this massive mixer. Dave developed his recipe almost 40 years ago, and it hasn't changed since; the Irish potato I ate when I was in the first grade is the same as the ones I ate right off the conveyor belt. "The only change was in the box. It used to be two pieces, and now it's just one." Once the mix is complete, it's put into the ball dropper, an elaborate machine that creates small balls of buttercream. These balls travel down a conveyor belt to the next section. The buttercream balls cascade off the conveyor belt and into a large tumbler that rolls them in cinnamon; it's a lot like a cement mixer filled with candy. "I call that The Cinnamonizer," Dave proclaimed proudly. After a healthy coating, the finished Irish Potatoes are popped out on the other side. The Irish potatoes are collected onto large trays and transported to the packaging portion of the process. This one extra batch represents approximately 850 pounds of candy. Now it's time for another conveyor belt trip. The potatoes move down this section and spill into the plastic containers that catch them on the other side. There are 15 potatoes in each box, a perfect number. "I had a dream one night of this device," Dave said. The production process reached such a high volume that he had to find new ways to innovate. With the help of a mechanically minded friend, this double-conveyor-belt system was born. Employees make sure the plastic shells are filled properly and then put them in the iconic white boxes. The next conveyor trip is for the plastic wrapping, which is done automatically by this massive machine. Alignment is very important here; if the boxes don't land in the exact right spot, there can be a big backup. And just like that, the Irish Potato box pops out on the other side! In the brief time I was there, I saw at least 100 boxes go through the process. Finally, the small white boxes are packaged into larger cases and ready to ship out to all the area Giants, Acmes, ShopRites, and many other stores that have customers clamoring for Irish Potatoes this time of year. If you look closely at the picture below, the wrapped towers of boxes are all complete cases. I'd estimate there were about 30 cases on each pallet. Mathies, do your work. While the process lacks the whimsy you'd find in Willy Wonka's candy factory, Dave's passion as a candy maker was evident throughout the tour. I asked him if he felt any pride that Oh Ryan's has become so synonymous with the Philadelphia area. "Whenever my wife and I go out for dinner, I bring a few boxes for the staff," Dave answered. "One time, I gave a box to our waitress, and she began to cry, saying her son had recently died and that Irish Potatoes were his favorite candy. And I think that's what it's all about: tradition. I like that people like them." And I like that too, Dave. If you're from this area of the country, you know what Dave is talking about. Folks around here are passionate about their food traditions, and Irish Potatoes are a big part of that. They just haven't cracked through on a national level. I hope more people get to create their own memories and traditions with Irish Potatoes in the future.
