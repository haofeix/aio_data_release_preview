Astronauts, despite the unique job title, are still government employees. In general, they get paid less than you might think. WASHINGTON — A team of four astronauts launched from NASA's Kennedy Space Center on Wednesday and are flying around the moon on a 10-day journey. But they won't be getting overtime for the trip. Despite their unique job title, NASA astronauts are still government employees, and their pay is less than you might think. U.S. astronauts are paid on a "General Services" pay scale, which is how federal officials calculate how much a salaried employee should make. For astronauts, according to NASA, that comes out to approximately $152,258 per year (as of 2024). That means pay is determined by three factors: the rigor of the job, how long they've been employed by the federal government, and their location. All NASA astronauts are technically based out of the Johnson Space Center in Houston, Texas. Most of them have been federal employees for at least 10 years, putting them at "Step 7" in the government's seniority ranking. And they're classified as GS-13 employees, some of the highest on the 15-point scale for determining job difficulty. According to FederalPay.org, which tracks government salaries, most astronauts don't actually earn as much as you might expect. Despite being classified as some of the highest-level government employees — astronauts are often military officers and scientists with advanced degrees — they can make less than $100,000 per year. FederalPay.org estimates NASA astronauts are often paid anywhere from $89,693 to $122,336 per year. The Canadian Space Agency, or CSA, has a relatively similar pay scale based on training and expertise: entry level, qualified level, and senior level. The salary scale for Levels I to III varies from $97,100 to $189,600, according to 2023 data. Senior level is designated for astronauts who have successfully completed a space mission. Despite the pay seeming low at first glance, being a NASA astronaut is one of the most coveted jobs in the world. More than 8,000 people applied when NASA last opened the selection process back in 2024. Currently, there are only 48 active U.S. astronauts. NASA has only ever hired 370 people in total since 1959 for the role. The rewards of the job, ranging from prestige and fame to the ability to leave the planet behind for a time, are enough to draw in thousands of applicants for whom money isn't the main factor. Do astronauts get overtime? The question of overtime became a public curiosity when astronauts Butch Willmore and Suni Williams became stuck aboard the International Space Station due to technical failures in the Boeing Starliner capsule that brought them there. The spacecraft had multiple helium leaks and thruster failures during its first crewed flight, prompting NASA to deem a return in the capsule too risky. Willmore and Williams spent 286 days in space, returning on March 18, 2025, after their planned eight-day mission was extended to nine months. But, they didn't receive any extra compensation for the delay in returning home. "When NASA astronauts are aboard the International Space Station, they receive regular, 40-hour....week salaries," NASA told People Magazine at the time. "They do not receive overtime or holiday/weekend pay." However, as government employees traveling for work, they did have their transportation (the rockets that got them to and from the ISS), lodging (aboard the permanently orbiting laboratory in Earth's upper atmosphere) and meals (yum) taken care of. What is Artemis II? Artemis II is the first crewed flight in the series of missions with the goal of returning astronauts to the moon. It will be the first trip to the moon in over 50 years, and NASA announced last week that the agency is setting a broader goal to establish a permanent base on the lunar surface. The world's first lunar visitors orbited the moon on Apollo 8 in 1968. It took NASA just eight years to go from putting its first astronaut in space to putting Apollo 11’s Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin on the moon in 1969, beating President John Kennedy’s end-of-decade deadline. Artemis has progressed much more slowly, after decades of indecision and flip-flopping between the moon and Mars as the next grand destination. NASA’s new moon rocket, the Space Launch System, or SLS, has soared only once in a test flight without anyone on board more than three years ago. The crew was announced in early April 2023, when the original goal was to launch in 2024. Three NASA astronauts and one Canadian Space Agency astronaut were selected for the mission. The mission has been delayed several times due to conditions and other technical challenges. But if all conditions hold, NASA hopes to finally launch the four astronauts into space Wednesday for the 10-day mission to orbit the moon.
