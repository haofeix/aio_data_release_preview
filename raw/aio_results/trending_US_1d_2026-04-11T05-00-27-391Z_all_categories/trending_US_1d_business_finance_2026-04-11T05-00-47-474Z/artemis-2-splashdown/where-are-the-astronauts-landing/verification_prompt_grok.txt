NASA's Artemis II mission has just completed its translunar injection burn — a key maneuver that has officially sent the astronauts zooming toward their lunar target. "We just wanted to communicate to everyone around the planet who's worked to make Artemis possible that we firmly felt the power of your perseverance during every second of that burn," Artemis II crew member Jeremy Hansen of the Canadian Space Agency said as their spacecraft sped away from Earth. Hansen, together with NASA astronauts Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover and Christina Koch, launched from Kennedy Space Center in Florida, ensconced in an Orion capsule attached to an SLS rocket. The historic mission will take them on a 230,000-mile journey to the lunar body that will serve as a critical test flight of the Orion spacecraft. The capsule is scheduled to fly by the moon on Monday. The nearly 10-day journey will not only test the spacecraft's life-support systems and maneuverability, but conduct critical science ahead of future deep space missions to the lunar surface. Here's what to expect over the course of the mission. The trajectory The mission is on a flight path that keeps the spacecraft in Earth's gravitational influence past the moon, then falls back to the planet for splashdown. This path, called a free return trajectory, uses less fuel and is less risky than entering a lunar orbit. NASA / This graphic shows key milestones along the Artemis II astronauts' journey around the moon and back. That's why on Thursday night the spacecraft performed the translunar injection burn, firing its engine for about five minutes and 50 seconds to send the Artemis II crew on their lunar journey. The maneuver is the point of no return, committing the crew to the lunar flyby. A failure would prevent the crew from reaching the moon. If it succeeds, onward they'll go. The path will take the crew to within about 5,000 miles above the lunar surface. Apollo missions typically orbited the moon under 100 miles (or touched down on the surface).
