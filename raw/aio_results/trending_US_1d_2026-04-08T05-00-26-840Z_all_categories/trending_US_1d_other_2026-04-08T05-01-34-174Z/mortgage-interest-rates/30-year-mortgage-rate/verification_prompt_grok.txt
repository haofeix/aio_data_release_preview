Factors you can control Your income, debt and credit score: Lenders give their best rates to borrowers who pose the least amount of risk. That means individuals with stable incomes, low debt-to-income (DTI) ratios and high credit scores. Improving your credit score before you apply for a mortgage can lead to lower overall costs. The spread: Mortgage rates don't exactly match the 10-year yield. There's what's called a “spread” between the two. This spread is typically around 2% to 3% on top of the 10-year yield. It’s not stagnant, though; it grows and contracts as lenders price in perceived risk. Federal Reserve decisions: The Fed doesn’t directly control mortgage rates, but their decisions can have a trickle-down effect. Mortgage lenders pay close attention to what the Fed says and does, and may respond by raising or lowering rates after Fed decisions. The global economy: Global trade issues, from conflicts to tariffs, can impact the decisions investors make. If this leads to buying or selling Treasury bonds, it will move mortgage rates.
